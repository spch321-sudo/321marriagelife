6	One Fruit, Nine Flavors
7	The fruit of the Spirit is one life, not nine assignments
8	Be still and picture a fruit tree quietly growing in the sunshine. It isn’t straining, yet it bears fruit naturally. Tell the Lord: “Holy Spirit, today I won’t rely on my own effort. I want to live in you and let you bear fruit in me.”
9	321 Light: We often try to “improve ourselves,” adding a little patience and a little gentleness to the old self, and we only grow more tired. The nine flavors of fruit are not character traits that the self-ruled build through effort; they are the life that grows naturally when the self-emptied let the Holy Spirit lead. Change your mindset and your life will change: shift from “I have to achieve this” to “Holy Spirit, bear this fruit in me.”
10	Jesus said, “the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing” (John 5:19). Every day the Son of God lived on earth, he lived in connection with the Father. Jesus is our example: fruit is not something we generate on our own; it flows out of an intimate relationship with the Father.
11	Holy Spirit, thank you for living in us. We confess that we often try to become better on our own, and the harder we try, the more tired we become. Today we hand our old selves over to the cross. Bear your whole fruit in us: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control, and make our home your garden. In the name of the Lord Jesus, amen.
12	Starting this week, we’ll spend nine weeks on “the fruit of the Spirit at home.” First, notice one detail: Paul speaks of “the fruit of the Spirit” (Galatians 5:22), and in the original, “fruit” is singular. Not nine fruits, but one fruit with nine flavors, like an orange with several segments that all belong to one orange. Love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith (or faithfulness), gentleness, and self-control are nine facets of one and the same life.
13	This matters. If we treat the nine as nine assignments, we’ll pick the ones we’re good at and let ourselves off the hook for the ones we lack: “I’m very patient; I’m just not very gentle.” But one fruit is whole. It isn’t a report card we piece together; it is what the life of Christ looks like as it grows in us. Jesus said, “I am the vine. You are the branches” (John 15:5). A branch doesn’t have to strain to squeeze out fruit; it only has to remain in the vine.
14	Paul continues: “Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and lusts” (Galatians 5:24). The fruit grows after the cross: once the old self has been put to death, the life of the Spirit has room. So Paul urges us, “If we live by the Spirit, let’s also walk by the Spirit” (Galatians 5:25). Home is the most real place for this fruit to grow.

23	My Spouse Is My Most Important “2”
24	To love our neighbor as ourselves, we start with the person right beside us
25	Close your eyes and think of the people you are connected to today, letting them come to mind one by one. Finally, let your thoughts rest on your spouse’s face. Gently tell the Lord: “Lord, thank you for placing them in the closest place in my life.”
26	321 Light: The order of relationships is God → self → others → tasks. The self-ruled treat their spouse as a tool for reaching their own goals, so tasks matter more than people; the self-emptied see their spouse as the “2” God has entrusted to them, so people matter more than tasks. Don’t let housework, money, or saving face hurt your most important “2.”
27	As soon as Jesus left the synagogue, he went into Simon’s house. Simon’s mother-in-law was lying sick with a fever, and Jesus “came and took her by the hand and raised her up” (Mark 1:31). After his public ministry before the crowds, Jesus brought healing into an ordinary home. Jesus shows us that his love is not only in the synagogue, but also behind the front door, with those closest to us.
28	Lord, thank you for placing us in the closest place in each other’s lives. We confess that we often give our best to outsiders and save our worst for our family. Forgive us for the times we have bitten and devoured each other. Help us serve one another in love, and let our spouse truly become our most important “2” on earth. Amen.
29	The 321 approach has a simple, memorable practice called 920 (my spouse is my most important “2”; practice the “0” of self-emptying; bear the “9”-fold fruit). Let’s spell it out this week: the “2” is every person I am in relationship with; the “0” is practicing the self-emptied life; the “9” is bearing the nine flavors of the fruit of the Spirit. The nine flavors aren’t developed alone on a mountaintop; they grow in relationships. Without the “2,” the “9” has nowhere to grow. And of all my “2”s, my spouse is my most important “2.”
30	Paul says, “through love be servants to one another. For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself’” (Galatians 5:13–14). A “neighbor” is someone near me. Who is nearer than our spouse? We share the same bed, the same dinner table, the same bank account. We are often patient with outsiders and polite to colleagues, but when we come home, we save our worst side for the person closest to us.
31	Paul also gives a warning: “But if you bite and devour one another, be careful that you don’t consume one another” (Galatians 5:15). This is a very realistic picture of marriage: one cutting remark bites, another bites back, nobody wins, and both get hurt. The practice of 920 starts right here: putting our spouse back in the place of “my most important 2,” serving them in love instead of biting them with our words.

40	The “0” of Self-Emptying: A Grain of Wheat Falls
41	Self-emptying is not losing yourself; it is bearing much fruit
42	Imagine holding a grain of wheat in your hand. The tighter you grip it, the more it stays just one grain. Slowly open your hand and tell the Lord: “Lord, I am willing to place the self I’ve been gripping so tightly into your hands.”
43	321 Light: The root of the problem is the self-ruled life: making ourselves the center, with self on the throne. We can’t get rid of self-rule by willpower; only through the gospel: Jesus’ death dealt with my sin and my old self, and Jesus’ resurrection gives me new life. So the “0” is not self-suppression; it is handing ourselves over to the Lord who died and rose again.
44	Right after speaking about the grain of wheat, Jesus said honestly, “Now my soul is troubled. What shall I say? ‘Father, save me from this time’? But I came to this time for this cause” (John 12:27). Jesus’ “0” didn’t mean having no feelings; in real struggle, he still chose the Father’s will. And because this grain of wheat fell into the ground, it bore the fruit of countless saved lives.
45	Lord Jesus, thank you for falling into the earth for us and bearing the fruit of salvation. We confess that we often grip ourselves tightly, afraid of losing out. Through your death, put away our old selves; through your resurrection, give us new life. Help us practice self-emptying at home, not as resentful suppression, but as hopeful surrender. Amen.
46	The “0” in 920 is practicing the self-emptied life. This “0” is often misunderstood as “I don’t matter” or “I have no voice.” But Jesus said, “unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit” (John 12:24). When a grain falls into the ground, it isn’t destroyed; it enters another form of life. Self-emptied does not mean empty; it means entering the limitless and living out of the limitless.
47	Why must the “9” pass through the “0”? Because for the self-ruled, every fruit first has to answer: “What’s in it for me?” I give love in order to be loved; I’m patient to prove I’m the better person. Such fruit actually “remains by itself alone.” Jesus said, “He who loves his life will lose it” (John 12:25). If we cling to our rights, our pride, and being right, the marriage is left with two hard seeds, each intact, but with no harvest.
48	In marriage, “falling into the earth” is often very ordinary: being the first to make peace even when you’re in the right; getting up to help your spouse finish something when you’d rather rest; letting go of “I told you so.” These little deaths grow unexpected fruit in the home: trust, warmth, laughter. The “0” is not the end; it is where the “9” begins.

57	Love: Walking in Love
58	Love is not a trade; it gives itself up, as Christ did
59	Picture a child watching their father work, then picking up a tool to copy him. Quietly tell the Lord: “Father, I am your beloved child. Today I want to learn from you how to love my spouse.”
60	321 Light: Self-ruled love is a trade: “If you treat me well, I’ll treat you well.” Pride provokes pride, and love as a trade ends up as mutual scorekeeping. Self-emptied love is an overflow: because God has already loved me enough, I can love first. Humility inspires humility, and the one who loves first often draws out love from the other.
61	Judas came with a crowd to arrest Jesus, using a kiss as the signal. Jesus said to him, “Friend, why are you here?” (Matthew 26:50). Facing the one who betrayed him, Jesus still called him “friend.” Jesus is our example: love doesn’t depend on whether the other person deserves it, because the source of love is in the Father.
62	Heavenly Father, thank you for loving us while we were still sinners and making us your beloved children. Our love often runs dry and often keeps score. Help us imitate you and walk in love, giving ourselves up for our spouse as Christ did, no longer loving as a trade, but loving first and loving always. Amen.
63	The first of the nine flavors is love. A Greek word often used in the New Testament, agape, does not mean love drawn out by attraction, nor love as a trade, but the kind of love God has: love that gives itself freely, whether or not the other person deserves it. Marriage needs romance and friendship too, but when the tide of romance goes out and friendship grows weary, it is this love that comes from God that holds a home together.
64	Paul says, “Be therefore imitators of God, as beloved children” (Ephesians 5:1). Children learn from their parents not by memorizing rules, but by watching and copying every day. Love is learned the same way: first knowing that we are God’s deeply loved children, and then learning to love the way our heavenly Father loves. Someone unsure of being loved often loves with strings attached; only someone sure of being loved can give freely.
65	This is what that love looks like: “Walk in love, even as Christ also loved us and gave himself up for us” (Ephesians 5:2). Paul says elsewhere, “while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8). God didn’t wait for us to improve before loving us. At home, love means treating our spouse well before they have changed, and choosing to love them even on a day when they are not lovable. Before God, such love is “a sweet-smelling fragrance” (Ephesians 5:2).

74	What Love Looks Like at Home
75	Love is not only in words but in deed and truth
76	Think back over every small thing you’ve done at home since this morning: a glass of water, a greeting, a glance. Quietly ask the Lord: “Lord, in these small things, where was your love? Where was it missing?”
77	321 Light: When life changes, daily living changes. If love stays only in our ideas, it hasn’t yet become life; if it stays only in our feelings, it hasn’t yet entered daily living. The practice of 920 has to take root in the small things of every day: life first, then relationship. Change begins with me, not with “waiting for my spouse to change.”
78	After his resurrection, Jesus made a charcoal fire on the shore, cooked fish and bread, and said to his tired disciples, “Come and eat breakfast!” (John 21:12). The risen Lord didn’t start with a sermon; he started by preparing breakfast. Jesus shows us that the deepest love is often hidden in the most everyday care.
79	Lord Jesus, thank you for laying down your life for us, so that we know what love is. Help us love not only with our lips, but in deed and truth. Let my spouse be my most important “2,” let me practice self-emptying in you, and let the Holy Spirit bear abundant fruit in our home, so that all who see our family recognize you. Amen.
80	John says, “let’s not love in word only, or with the tongue only, but in deed and truth” (1 John 3:18). Many couples say “I love you” easily, yet rarely ask: what does my love actually look like? Love at home is usually not some dramatic sacrifice. It is a glass of warm water, a pair of socks washed and put away, a “Thanks for all your hard work today,” and after an argument, being the first to climb down.
81	John first names the source of love: “By this we know love, because he laid down his life for us” (1 John 3:16). Love is defined not by my feelings but by the cross. Jesus also said, “Just as I have loved you, you also love one another” (John 13:34). “Just as” is key: not in the way I’m used to, but in the way Jesus loves me, I love my most important “2.”
82	This week we have seen the whole picture of 920: my spouse is my most important “2,” I practice the “0” of self-emptying, and I let the Holy Spirit bear the “9” flavors of fruit. Love is the first flavor, and the root of the other eight, nourishing the whole tree like a root system. Over the next eight weeks, we’ll taste one flavor each week. There is no need to rush for it all to grow at once. Fruit needs seasons, and we’ll grow slowly together.
