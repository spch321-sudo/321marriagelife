0	Love Is Patient and Kind
1	A Little Surprise of Kindness
2	Before you head out, each secretly prepare a small surprise that costs little but that your spouse will enjoy: their favorite drink, a handwritten card, or taking care of some small task they’ve been putting off for ages.
3	Exchange surprises on your date and share: “This week, you were really patient with me about… Thank you.”
4	Together, come up with a plan to show kindness to someone else this month, such as taking a lonely friend out for a meal.
5	Hold hands, read the first half of 1 Corinthians 13:4 together, and pray for each other.
6	Without Love, I Am Nothing
7	However much we give, without love all that’s left is noise
8	Be still and think about what you did for your family today. Don’t rush to grade yourself; just tell the Lord: “Lord, I’ve done a lot, but I want to know: is there love in any of it?”
9	321 Light: Giving without love is often self-ruled giving: I’ve done so much, so you must be grateful and you must notice me. When the focus is on myself, giving turns into collecting a debt. When we let Jesus reign, giving becomes simple again: for the Lord and for our spouse, not for my own record of merit.
10	Martha was busy serving Jesus, but she was frazzled inside and even complained about her sister. Jesus said gently, “Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is needed” (Luke 10:41–42). Jesus wasn’t dismissing her service; he was bringing Martha back to the heart behind it.
11	Lord, we confess that we often do a great deal while our hearts have no love left, only weariness and scorekeeping. Forgive us for treating our giving as a record of merit. Put your love back into our hearts, so that every small thing we do for each other becomes a melody of love rather than noise. Amen.
12	Picture a beautifully designed phone with a big screen and multiple cameras, but a dead battery. However good it looks, it can’t make a single call. The church in Corinth had many gifts, such as tongues, prophecy, and knowledge, yet they compared themselves with each other and split into factions. Right between two chapters on spiritual gifts, Paul placed this chapter, saying, “I show a most excellent way to you” (1 Corinthians 12:31). That most excellent way is love.
13	Paul says, “If I speak with the languages of men and of angels, but don’t have love, I have become sounding brass or a clanging cymbal” (1 Corinthians 13:1). A gong and a cymbal are loud, but they carry no melody. He goes on to say that even with all faith, even “If I give away all my goods to feed the poor... but don’t have love, it profits me nothing” (1 Corinthians 13:3). However impressive our outward actions, without love they are empty in God’s eyes.
14	This passage is very practical for couples. We may spend every day driving the kids around, cooking, working overtime, and paying bills, doing lots of “right things,” while our hearts are full of resentment and scorekeeping. What our spouse hears is not our giving, but the noise of gongs and cymbals. This week we’ll read the portrait of love line by line. The first step is to ask honestly: is there still love in my giving?
15	Of the things I’ve done for my family recently, which one has turned into “sounding brass or a clanging cymbal”?
16	Do I often keep a mental tally of how much I have given to this family?
17	Of the things I do for our home, which one makes you feel most loved? Which one do you sense I do out of exhaustion or reluctance? Please tell me gently.
18	If our family life were a piece of music, would you say it sounds more like a melody or more like noise right now? How could we tune it?
19	Pick one daily household chore and do it today with prayer and love
20	Tell your spouse: “Thank you for doing … for our family. I see it.”
21	Say one less complaint today
22	“but don’t have love, I am nothing” — Lord, add your love to everything I do right now.
23	A Long Fuse
24	Patience means being slow to anger, as God is
25	Take three deep breaths, each one a little slower than the last. Tell the Lord: “Lord, you have been so patient with me. Today, please put your long-suffering patience in my heart.”
26	321 Light: A short fuse often comes from too big a “me”: my time, my standards, and my feelings must never be offended. The self-emptied have a longer fuse, because they remember that God’s patience with me far exceeds my patience with my spouse.
27	Philip had followed Jesus for so long, yet he still said, “Show us the Father.” Jesus answered, “Have I been with you such a long time, and do you not know me, Philip?” (John 14:9). There was a sigh in his voice, but he did not give up; he patiently taught once more. Jesus is our example of patience.
28	Merciful and gracious God, thank you that you are slow to anger and so full of patience toward us. We confess that our fuses with each other are often too short, and we explode at the slightest spark. Holy Spirit, give us a quiet space between our anger and our reactions, so that your love can speak first. Amen.
29	A firecracker has a fuse. With a short fuse, it explodes the moment it’s lit; with a long fuse, there’s still time to put the flame out after it’s lit. Paul says, “Love is patient” (1 Corinthians 13:4). In the original Greek, this is a verb that literally carries the sense of “being long-tempered,” bearing with others for a long time and being slow to anger. Patient people aren’t people without fire; they simply have a long fuse that leaves room for love.
30	This pattern comes from God himself. After Israel worshiped the golden calf, God proclaimed before Moses: “The LORD! The LORD, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth” (Exodus 34:6). In Hebrew, “slow to anger” pictures anger that is long in coming. Israel had just betrayed him, and yet this is how God introduced himself.
31	What tests our patience most in marriage is usually not the big things but the small, repeated ones: saying the same thing for the tenth time, seeing the same habit show up again. Patience is not pretending everything is fine; it is leaving a space between our anger and our reaction, so the Holy Spirit has a chance to speak. That space may be only three seconds, yet it often decides the direction of the whole conversation.
32	With which of my spouse’s small habits is my “fuse” especially short?
33	When I think about how long God has been patient with me, what do I feel?
34	When I’m about to lose my temper, how do you think I could pause for three seconds first? Could we agree on a signal to remind each other?
35	In what area do you most appreciate my patience with you?
36	When you feel like losing your temper today, pause for three seconds and say in your heart, “Lord”
37	Agree with your spouse on a gentle “time-out” signal
38	Write down one way God has been patient with you
39	“a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger” — Lord, right now lengthen my fuse, and let love speak first.
40	Bearing Each Other’s Weaknesses
41	Learning to accept our spouse’s weaknesses and habits rather than remake them
42	Think of one of your spouse’s habits that often makes you sigh. Don’t rush to pray “Lord, change them.” Instead, quietly say: “Lord, this is how you have accepted my weaknesses too.”
43	321 Light: Trying to remake my spouse to fit my standards is trying to take God’s place in doing his work in them. Let people be people, and let God be God: my part is to accept and to bear; God’s part is to change them. Humility inspires humility, and a person who feels accepted actually finds it easier to change.
44	The prophet described Jesus this way: “He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax” (Matthew 12:20). When Jesus faced weak people, he did not snap them in two; he carefully lifted them up. He was gentle toward weakness, yet he never compromised with sin. This is the direction for how we learn patience.
45	Lord Jesus, thank you for accepting us while we were still weak. Help us accept each other and bear each other’s weaknesses, instead of rushing to remake each other. And please protect everyone who is hurt or afraid in their marriage; give them safety, wisdom, and help. Amen.
46	There was once a couple: the wife liked to squeeze the toothpaste from the end, while the husband always squeezed it from the middle. They argued over this little thing for years. Eventually the wife simply bought two tubes, one each, and they both laughed. Marriage is full of weaknesses and habits that aren’t sins, just differences. They are not enemies to be wiped out, but weights to be carried.
47	Paul says, “we who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak, and not to please ourselves” (Romans 15:1). To “bear” means to pick something up and carry it. He also says, “accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you” (Romans 15:7). Christ did not accept us after we got better, but while we were still weak. Acceptance doesn’t mean approving of everything; it means first welcoming the person in.
48	We need to be especially clear here: bearing with weakness does not mean putting up with harm. Physical, verbal, sexual, or financial violence and control are not “weaknesses,” and they are not something love is meant to tolerate. If you feel afraid or unsafe in your marriage, first protect your own safety and your children’s, and seek help from a pastor and from professionals. Seeking help is not a failure of patience; it is an expression of love and truth.
49	What “difference” in my spouse have I kept treating as a mistake to be corrected?
50	Which of my own weaknesses has my spouse been quietly bearing for a long time?
51	Which of my little habits often requires patience from you? Please tell me in a lighthearted way.
52	Is there some small thing we keep tugging back and forth over that we could solve with a “two tubes of toothpaste” approach?
53	Choose to smile rather than comment on one of your spouse’s little habits today
54	Tell your spouse: “Thank you for putting up with my…”
55	If you feel unsafe in your marriage, contact a trusted pastor or professional today
56	“accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you” — Lord, right now I choose to accept my spouse just as you have accepted me.
57	Kindness Leads Us Home
58	Kindness is actively doing good, and it changes people more than rebuke does
59	Recall how God once gently brought you back when you had stumbled. Quietly thank him: “Lord, it was your kindness that turned me around.”
60	321 Light: Trying to change my spouse through blame means taking the judge’s seat, and that is leaving our rightful place. God’s view is that kindness leads people to repentance. When I lay down the power of “I’m right and you’re wrong,” return to my rightful place as a servant, and actively treat my spouse well, God works in their heart.
61	The crowd wanted to stone a woman caught in adultery, but Jesus said, “Neither do I condemn you. Go your way. From now on, sin no more” (John 8:11). He did not take sin lightly, but he first protected her with kindness and then called her to a new life. Kindness and truth are perfectly joined in Jesus.
62	Our Savior, thank you that your kindness and love came to us and saved us when we were least lovable. Forgive us for so often treating each other with blame. Make us warm like the sun, opening each other’s hearts by actively doing good, and let your kindness lead people home in our family. Amen.
63	The North Wind and the Sun made a bet over who could get a traveler to take off his coat. The North Wind blew with all its might, and the traveler only wrapped his coat more tightly around himself; the Sun shone warmly, and the traveler took off his coat on his own. This fable is a lot like marriage: blame often makes people more defensive, while kindness helps them lower their guard. The harder we try to “blow” our spouse awake, the more they tend to wrap themselves up.
64	Paul asks, “do you despise the riches of his goodness, forbearance, and patience, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?” (Romans 2:4). The word WEB renders “goodness” here is God’s kindness. God’s way of changing us is not to condemn first, but to show kindness first. In 1 Corinthians 13:4, “kind” is also a verb, meaning to actively treat others well and do what is truly good for them. Patience is not exploding; kindness goes a step further: it takes the initiative to do good.
65	Titus describes what we once were: “hateful, and hating one another” (Titus 3:3), and then says, “when the kindness of God our Savior and his love toward mankind appeared... he saved us” (Titus 3:4–5). We are all people changed by kindness. At home, a glass of warm water, a word like “Thanks for all your hard work,” or an understanding look can often open our spouse’s heart more than ten good arguments.
66	When my spouse does something wrong, do I usually respond more like the North Wind or the Sun?
67	What act of God’s kindness once changed my heart?
68	When you’ve done something wrong or you’re in a bad mood, what could I do that would make you feel most kindly treated?
69	Was there a recent time when I used blame instead of kindness? I’d like to apologize, and I’d like to hear how you felt.
70	Today, take the initiative to do something good for your spouse that they didn’t ask for
71	Swap one critical comment you want to make for an understanding one
72	Do a small act of kindness for the family member you find hardest to treat kindly
73	“the goodness of God leads you to repentance” — Lord, right now I choose to treat my family with your kindness.
74	Always at the King’s Table
75	Kind love prepares a place for the other person
76	Imagine being invited to sit at a long table, where the host personally pulls out a chair for you. Look back over this week: love in our giving, a long fuse, bearing weaknesses, kindness. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, thank you for preparing a place for me.”
77	321 Light: David showed kindness because he remembered a covenant, not because of what the other person had to offer. Marriage, too, is a covenant, not a transaction. Relationships matter more than things, and people matter more than tasks: a home where a weak person can sit down and feel safe is a home being built as a household that belongs to God.
78	When the disciples were so busy they didn’t even have time to eat, Jesus said, “Come away into a deserted place, and rest awhile” (Mark 6:31). Jesus saw how tired people were and took the initiative to make space for them to rest. This is what Jesus’ kindness looks like: not only making demands, but showing care.
79	King of covenant love, thank you for seeking us because of your covenant, so that weak people like us can always eat at your table. Make our home a table of kindness, where each other’s weaknesses have a place to rest. May we never give up in patience, and may we actively do good in kindness, showing your love. Amen.
80	After David became king, he asked a remarkable question: “Is there not yet any of Saul’s house, that I may show the kindness of God to him?” (2 Samuel 9:3). By the custom of the time, a new king would often wipe out the descendants of the previous dynasty. But David remembered his covenant with Jonathan and went looking on his own initiative. The word translated “kindness” here is the Hebrew word for covenant love: faithfulness and goodness that flow from a covenant.
81	The one they found was Mephibosheth, who at five years old had fallen while fleeing and was lame for the rest of his life. When he came before David, he fell on his face, terrified. Yet David said, “Don’t be afraid,” and also, “You will eat bread at my table continually” (2 Samuel 9:7). Kindness is not just helping out once; it is keeping a lasting place at your own table for someone who is weak.
82	This week we have learned about patience and kindness. Patience means not giving up; kindness means actively doing good. Mephibosheth’s legs didn’t get better, but he always ate at the king’s table. In marriage, our spouse also carries their own “lameness”: past wounds, the limits of their personality. Love doesn’t wait for them to be healed before accepting them; it lets them know: at this family’s table, there will always be a place for you.
83	What “lameness” in my spouse do I keep hoping will get better before I’m willing to accept them?
84	Of this week’s lessons on patience and kindness, where does God most want to work in me?
85	In our home, when do you most feel that you “have a place” and are welcome?
86	Could we do something together to make our dinner table more of a place of kindness? For example, putting our phones away at meals and checking in with each other.
87	At dinner tonight, put your phone away and really look at your spouse as you talk
88	Tell your spouse: “Whatever happens, there will always be a place for you in this family.”
89	Memorize the first half of 1 Corinthians 13:4
90	“You will eat bread at my table continually” — Lord, right now I thank you for the place you have prepared for me.
