0	Love Never Fails
1	A Four-Season Map of Our Marriage
2	Take a sheet of paper and divide it into four boxes labeled spring, summer, autumn, and winter. Together, think back over the seasons your marriage has passed through, and each write a few memorable events in the matching boxes.
3	Take turns sharing: “In that winter, what kept us from letting go?” Notice God’s faithfulness in every season.
4	Each name one hope for the future, write it on the back of the paper, and pray over it together.
5	Read 1 Corinthians 13:4–8a aloud together, then read it again replacing “love” with “the Lord Jesus,” and ask him to live out this kind of love in you.
6	Bears All Things: The Roof Over Our Home
7	Love is like a roof, sheltering our spouse from wind and rain, but never covering up harm
8	Imagine sitting at home on a rainy night, listening to the rain drum on the roof while you stay dry and warm. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, thank you for being my shelter. Make me a shelter for my family too.”
9	321 Light: Exposing our spouse’s faults is often a way to prove “I’m better,” and that is the pride of the self-ruled life. Self-emptied love is willing to bear the weight and protect our spouse’s dignity, because we are one; their shame is my shame. Relationships matter more than saving face, and people matter more than being right.
10	When the soldiers came to arrest Jesus, he stepped forward and said, “If therefore you seek me, let these go their way” (John 18:8). Jesus placed himself in front of his disciples, protecting them like a roof. True bearing means covering the ones we love with ourselves.
11	Lord, thank you for being our shelter and covering our many sins. Make us a roof for each other, never exposing each other’s faults, but shielding each other from the storm in times of weakness. And please protect everyone who is being harmed in their home; give them courage to speak up, and lead them to people who can help. Amen.
12	One of the most important parts of a house is the roof. When storms come, the roof doesn’t keep a tally of every raindrop; it simply keeps the rain out so the people inside can sleep in peace. Paul says love “bears all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7). The original word carries the sense of covering and bearing up, close to the image of a roof. Love is like a roof: when someone in the family is weak, it shields them from the storm outside.
13	Proverbs says, “Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all wrongs” (Proverbs 10:12). Peter also says, “above all things be earnest in your love among yourselves, for love covers a multitude of sins” (1 Peter 4:8). Covering means not broadcasting our spouse’s weaknesses, not exposing their faults to friends and family or on social media, and not belittling them in front of the children. Love protects our spouse’s reputation as if it were our own.
14	But a roof keeps out the rain outside; it doesn’t hide a fire inside the house. Bearing all things does not mean keeping secrets for violence, affairs, addiction, or any ongoing harm. If there is physical, verbal, sexual, or financial violence and control in the home, it is not something to be covered; it needs to be brought into the light and dealt with. Please put safety first and seek help from a pastor and from professionals. This is not betrayal; it is true love.
15	Have I ever told others about my spouse’s weaknesses or embarrassing moments? What was I hoping to gain at the time?
16	How has God covered my wrongs and kept me from being shamed in front of others?
17	Was there a time when I talked about you in front of others and it made you uncomfortable? Please tell me. I want to learn to protect you better.
18	Is there a pressure or weakness you’re facing lately that you’d like me to quietly help carry?
19	Say something good about your spouse in front of others today
20	Stop complaining about your spouse on social media or in chats
21	Quietly take on one task to share a pressure your spouse is facing
22	“love covers a multitude of sins” — Lord, right now make me a shelter for my family.
23	Believes All Things: Seeing What God Is Doing
24	Love is not naive; it believes in God’s good work in our spouse
25	Think about what your spouse was like when you first met, and what they are like now. Quietly ask the Lord: “Lord, what good work have you done in them that I haven’t noticed?”
26	321 Light: Labeling my spouse with “That’s just how they are” is me drawing conclusions in God’s place. Let God be God: admit that he understands my spouse better than I do and is more able to change them than I am. When our focus is on Jesus, we can turn from disappointment to hope, and from judgment to intercession.
27	Before Peter ever fell, Jesus told him, “but I prayed for you, that your faith wouldn’t fail. You, when once you have turned again, establish your brothers” (Luke 22:32). Jesus knew Peter would fail, yet he already believed Peter would turn back. That is love that believes all things.
28	God who completes every good work, thank you that you have never given up on us, nor on our spouse. Remove the labels and judgments from our hearts, and give us eyes of faith to see the work you are doing in each other. Let us strengthen each other with “I believe in you,” and wait together for you to complete your work. Amen.
29	A sculptor looks at a rough block of stone. Others see only stone, but he already sees the statue inside. Love “believes all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7). That doesn’t mean love is naive and believes anything; it means love is willing to see people with the eyes of faith, seeing not only who they are now, but also who God is shaping them to become.
30	Paul says, “being confident of this very thing, that he who began a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ” (Philippians 1:6). Notice that the focus of Paul’s confidence is not people but God. Believing in our spouse doesn’t mean believing they will never fail again; it means believing God hasn’t given up on them and is still at work in them. This lets us keep faith in our spouse even in disappointment.
31	Peter denied the Lord three times, yet after the resurrection Jesus asked him three times whether he loved him, and three times entrusted him with the charge: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17). Jesus didn’t label Peter “unreliable” because of his failure; instead he believed in him again and entrusted him with a task. Love that believes all things gives our spouse the chance to start again, and rebuilds them with three words: “I believe in you.”
32	Have I ever put a label on my spouse, such as “They’ll just never change”?
33	What good work has God already done in my spouse that I can be thankful for?
34	In what area would you most like me to have more faith in you?
35	Name one change you have seen God make in each other, and tell your spouse: “I believe God will keep completing it.”
36	Tell your spouse today: “I believe God has a good plan for you.”
37	Write down three ways your spouse has grown in recent years, and thank God
38	Tear off one negative label you have stuck on your spouse in your heart
39	“he who began a good work in you will complete it” — Lord, right now I believe you will complete your work in our home.
40	Hopes All Things: A Door in the Valley of Trouble
41	God can turn the valley of trouble in a marriage into a door of hope
42	Think of a low point your marriage has passed through, or is passing through now. You don’t need to hurry out of it; just quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, in this valley, open a door for us.”
43	321 Light: We lose hope when our eyes are fixed on our spouse or our circumstances. The earthly view sees only the valley; the heavenly view sees the door. Moving from the temporary to the eternal, with our focus on Jesus, we can keep preparing for spring even in winter.
44	Jairus’ daughter had died and everyone was weeping, but Jesus said, “The child is not dead, but is asleep” (Mark 5:39). People laughed at him, but he took the child by the hand and raised her up. Where everyone else saw only despair, Jesus saw hope.
45	God of hope, thank you that you can turn the valley of trouble into a door of hope. We place the low points and disappointments of our marriage in your hands. Speak tenderly to us in the wilderness, fill our hearts with joy and peace as we trust in you, and let us overflow with hope by the power of the Holy Spirit. Amen.
46	In winter, a tree loses all its leaves and looks dead. But anyone who knows gardening knows that the roots are quietly gathering strength underground, waiting for spring. Love “hopes all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7). It means believing, even in the winter of a marriage, that spring will come, because our hope is in God, not in our circumstances.
47	The book of Hosea uses marriage to describe the relationship between God and Israel. The people had abandoned God, yet God said, “I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her,” and “I will give her... the valley of Achor for a door of hope” (Hosea 2:14–15). “Achor” means “trouble”; it was the place where Israel was punished because of Achan’s sin. God actually turns the most painful valley into a door of hope.
48	Paul blesses the church: “may the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing” (Romans 15:13). The source of hope is God. Hope in marriage is not cheerfully pretending there are no problems; it is fixing our eyes, in the midst of problems, on the God who raises the dead. For many couples, a low point later became the starting point of a deeper connection.
49	Is there a “valley of Achor” in our marriage that I hardly dare to hope about anymore?
50	Is my hope right now fixed more on my spouse changing, or on God?
51	Think back to a low point we went through. How did God later open a door in it?
52	What do you hope for our marriage in the coming year? If your spouse can’t join you for now, write down the hopes you hold for them and for your family.
53	Write down something you have given up hoping for, and hand it to God again
54	Tell your spouse: “I have hope for our future.”
55	Remember a door God opened in one of your low points, and thank him for it
56	“the valley of Achor for a door of hope” — Lord, right now open a door of hope for us in our valley.
57	Endures All Things: Waiting for the Early and Late Rain
58	Love can stand firm under heavy loads, like a farmer waiting for the harvest
59	Picture a farmer standing at the edge of his field. The seed is sown, but nothing has broken the surface yet. He doesn’t anxiously dig up the soil; he simply waits quietly. Tell the Lord: “Lord, teach me to wait for you like this in my marriage too.”
60	321 Light: We lose patience when we want to harvest on our own timetable; enduring all things means letting God be in charge of time. When we live from the heart by the Spirit, we have lasting strength; when we live from the head by ourselves, we easily start counting the cost and give up too soon.
61	In the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus prayed in anguish, “Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done” (Luke 22:42). He did not run from the burden; under its weight he surrendered to the Father and walked the road to the cross to the end. This is love that endures all things.
62	Lord, you are the God who holds time in your hands. We confess that we often want to see change on our own timetable, and we lose heart when it doesn’t come. Strengthen our hearts, so that like a farmer waiting for the early and late rain, we keep being faithful in small things. Let love give our waiting meaning, and keep us standing firm under heavy loads. Amen.
63	In Week 10 we read that love “is patient,” which is about being slow to anger with people. Here, “endures all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7) uses a different word, which in the original carries the sense of standing firm under a heavy load and holding on to the end. The first is like a long fuse; the second is like a bridge pier that stays in place however hard the current pounds it. Marriage needs both kinds of patience.
64	James says, “the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it, until it receives the early and late rain” (James 5:7). A farmer can’t make the rain come early, but he can keep plowing, weeding, and waiting. The winter of a marriage is like this too: some changes can’t be seen in a week or two. What we can do is keep being faithful in small things, trusting that God will send the rain in his time.
65	Jacob served seven years to marry Rachel, and the Bible says, “They seemed to him but a few days, for the love he had for her” (Genesis 29:20). Love changed how the time felt. That doesn’t mean the waiting wasn’t hard; it means love gave the waiting meaning. Love that endures all things isn’t gritting your teeth and hanging on; it holds on because it sees that the other person is worth it. But please remember: endurance never means anyone should stay in violence or danger.
66	In my marriage, what am I expecting to change “quickly”? Have I given God time?
67	Is my endurance more like gritting my teeth and hanging on, or holding on because I see it’s worth it?
68	Is there something in our marriage that we need to wait for patiently together? How can we encourage each other while we wait?
69	At what moment did my patience with you make you feel loved?
70	Write down one thing that calls for patient waiting, and pray about it every day
71	Today, keep being faithful in one small thing without asking about the results
72	Tell your spouse: “Thank you for being so patient with me all along.”
73	“You also be patient. Establish your hearts” — Lord, right now strengthen my heart to keep loving while I wait.
74	Love Never Fails
75	Everything else will pass away; only love remains forever
76	Look back over these four weeks: the source of love; patience and kindness; not seeking our own way; bearing, believing, hoping, and enduring all things. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, I can’t do any of this love on my own, but you are love. Live it out in me.”
77	321 Light: Our love fails because human love is limited; God’s love never fails because God is limitless. Self-emptied does not mean empty; it means entering the limitless and living out of the limitless. When we stop loving in our own strength and let Christ love through us, a limited husband and wife can live out a love that never fails.
78	Before the Last Supper, John describes Jesus this way: “having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end” (John 13:1). The disciples were about to desert him, yet he loved them to the end: to the cross, and forever. Jesus himself is the love that never fails.
79	Love that never fails, we thank you, for you are love. We confess that we cannot live out this kind of love on our own, but you love us to the end. Dwell in our home, so that our patience, kindness, bearing, believing, and hoping all come from you. May our marriage become a testimony of your love until the day we see you face to face. Amen.
80	When we got married, we received many gifts: the appliances will wear out, the photos will yellow, even the house will grow old. But Paul says, “Love never fails” (1 Corinthians 13:8). Prophecy, tongues, and knowledge, the gifts the Corinthians were so proud of, “will be done away with,” but love will not. Love is the only thing we can carry into eternity.
81	Paul uses two pictures. One is a child growing up: “Now that I have become a man, I have put away childish things” (1 Corinthians 13:11). The other is a mirror: “For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12). Ancient mirrors were polished bronze, and the image was blurry. Today our knowledge of God and of our spouse is limited, but one day we will see face to face and know fully.
82	Finally Paul sums it up: “But now faith, hope, and love remain—these three. The greatest of these is love” (1 Corinthians 13:13). Over these four weeks, we started from the source of love and learned the shape of love line by line. Perhaps we have discovered that only Jesus has ever fully lived out these descriptions of love. So the practice of love is not trying hard to imitate it, but letting the Love that never fails live in our home.
83	Of these four weeks on the nature of love, which verse has most changed how I see love?
84	If I replace “love” in 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 with my own name, which line can I hardly bear to read? I want to hand it to the Lord.
85	Over these four weeks, in what specific way have you seen my love grow? Please tell me.
86	If our family were to choose one “family motto of love” from 1 Corinthians 13, which line would you choose? Why?
87	Choose a “family motto of love” with your spouse, write it down, and post it in your home
88	Read 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 aloud, replacing “love” with “Jesus”
89	Memorize 1 Corinthians 13:7
90	“Love never fails” — Lord, right now let your never-failing love live in our home.
