0	Fruit of the Spirit (3): Peace
1	An Evening of Tearing Down a Wall
2	Get a few building blocks or sticky notes. Each of you writes down one thing that has recently “put up a little wall” between you (start with “I,” and don’t write accusations).
3	Take turns sharing. The one listening responds only with: “Thank you for telling me. I hear you.”
4	Together, knock the blocks over or tear up the notes, and say: “Christ has broken down the middle wall of separation.”
5	Holding hands, read Colossians 3:15 aloud and ask that the peace Christ gives would rule in our hearts.
6	Let Christ’s Peace Rule
7	Peace in the home begins with who is ruling in our hearts
8	Place your hand on your chest and feel your heartbeat. Quietly ask: “Right now, what is ruling my heart: anxiety, anger, or the peace of Christ?” Then say to the Lord: “Lord, please take the throne of my heart.”
9	321 Light: A home often lacks peace because it has two kings: I want to be in charge, and so do you. Pride against pride, and both lose. When we let Jesus reign, husband and wife both return to our rightful place, and peace has a foundation. Whoever rules in the heart rules in the home.
10	When the storm raged, Jesus was in the stern, “asleep on the cushion” (Mark 4:38). He woke and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” (Mark 4:39). His calm did not come from the absence of wind and waves, but from complete trust in the Father. Jesus had peace within first, and that brought calm without.
11	Lord of peace, thank you for leaving us your peace. We confess that we often let anger, anxiety, and our own opinions rule our hearts. Today we hand the throne of our hearts back to you. Let your peace be the umpire within us, so that as husband and wife we stop fighting over who is in charge and instead submit to you together. Amen.
12	Paul writes, “let the peace of God rule in your hearts” (Colossians 3:15). The word translated “rule” originally meant “to act as umpire,” like a referee on the field who blows the final whistle. When two voices argue inside us—“I’m going to snap back” and “I’m going to stay quiet”—who blows the whistle? Paul says: let God’s peace, the peace Christ gives, be the referee.
13	This peace is different from what the world gives. Jesus said, “My peace I give to you; not as the world gives, I give to you” (John 14:27). The world’s peace is conditional: I’ll have peace once the problem is solved, once my spouse apologizes. Christ’s peace is relational: because he is with me, even before the problem is solved there is already an unshakable center in my heart. Peace in the home has to begin with peace in each heart.
14	Just before this, Paul says, “Above all these things, walk in love, which is the bond of perfection” (Colossians 3:14). Last week’s love and joy and this week’s peace are really one fruit. He also says we “were called in one body.” A husband and wife are called to become one—not to fight over who is in charge, but together to let Jesus reign.
15	Lately, when my spouse and I disagree, what usually “blows the whistle” in my heart: anger, fear, or the peace of Christ?
16	Does my peace usually have to wait until my spouse changes first or apologizes first?
17	When do you feel our home has the most peace? When is it most tense?
18	When peace starts to slip away in our hearts, what short phrase could we use to remind each other to “let Christ’s peace rule”?
19	Today, when emotions rise, pause for three seconds and pray silently: “Lord, you rule.”
20	Tell your spouse: “Today I want to let Christ’s peace rule.”
21	Before bed, read John 14:27 together
22	“let the peace of God rule in your hearts” — Lord, right now let your peace blow the whistle and rule in my heart.
23	Breaking Down the Dividing Wall
24	The wall the cross tore down—let’s not build it back up
25	Picture a brick wall standing between you and your spouse, each brick inscribed with something never talked through. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, you know which bricks are between us. Help us take them down one by one.”
26	321 Light: The wall is actually built from the self-ruled life: my grievances, my pride, my being right. Every brick is there to protect me. Only the self-emptied can become one: when I’m willing to remove the bricks on my side first, the wall starts to shrink. Change starts with me, not with the other person.
27	When Jesus was arrested, the disciples scattered, and Peter denied him three times. Yet the risen Jesus “himself stood among them, and said to them, ‘Peace be to you’” (Luke 24:36). He didn’t settle accounts first; he tore down the wall first. Jesus shows us that the first step toward reconciliation is often taken by the one who was hurt.
28	Lord Jesus, thank you for tearing down the wall between us and God on the cross, and for making us as husband and wife one. Wherever walls have quietly gone up between us, give us courage to take them down together. Let us stop protecting our pride and start protecting our oneness. Amen.
29	Paul says Jesus “is our peace, who made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation” (Ephesians 2:14). In the temple of that day there really was a wall that kept Gentiles out. The hostility between Jews and Gentiles ran deeper than any conflict between spouses today, yet the cross made these two groups into “one new man” (Ephesians 2:15). If Christ could tear down a wall that thick, the walls in our home can come down too.
30	The wall between a husband and wife usually isn’t built in a day. An argument with no apology, a need that was overlooked, a disappointment never voiced—each one is a brick. Over time, two people live under the same roof yet seem to live on opposite sides of a wall. Cold silences, each scrolling our own phone, talking only about the kids and the bills—these are signs the wall has grown high.
31	Paul says Christ came to “reconcile them both in one body to God through the cross, having killed the hostility through it” (Ephesians 2:16). The foundation of peace is not which of us communicates better, but that we have both been reconciled to God. When I remember how God tore down the wall between himself and me, I become more willing to remove my brick first. Peace doesn’t mean waiting for the wall to fall on its own; it means taking it down together.
32	Is there a “brick” between my spouse and me that hasn’t been removed yet? What is written on it?
33	Am I waiting for my spouse to tear down the wall first, or am I willing to start? What makes me hesitate?
34	Do you sense a small wall that has slowly gone up between us lately? How could we take down the first brick together?
35	When there’s distance between us, how would you most like me to come close to you?
36	Today, gently bring up a small matter you’ve never talked through
37	At dinner tonight, put your phones in another room
38	Apologize to your spouse for a “little wall” you once put up
39	“broke down the middle wall of separation” — Lord, right now I am willing to remove the bricks on my side first.
40	True Peace, Not False Peace
41	Peace is not the absence of conflict but the ability to reconcile
42	Ask yourself honestly: “In our home, is there something we deliberately avoid mentioning, as if it doesn’t exist?” Quietly place it before the Lord. You don’t need to rush to fix it—just admit that it’s there.
43	321 Light: False peace is often the self-ruled life in another form: fear of conflict is really fear of being hurt or losing face. True peace takes humility: willingness to speak the truth and willingness to hear it. Proverbs says, “Better is a dry morsel with quietness, than a house full of feasting with strife” (Proverbs 17:1), because people matter more than issues.
44	Jesus taught, “If your brother sins against you, go, show him his fault between you and him alone” (Matthew 18:15). Jesus didn’t tell us to pretend nothing happened. He told us to deal with it privately, directly, and with a heart to restore—so that “you have gained back your brother,” not so that you win the argument.
45	Righteous and loving Lord, we don’t want false peace; we want your true peace. Give us courage to speak the truth and humility to hear it. Heal with your own hand every wound that has been covered up in our home. If anyone is in danger, protect them, open a way, and lead them to help. Amen.
46	God rebuked the leaders of that day: “They have healed also the hurt of my people superficially, saying, ‘Peace, peace!’ when there is no peace” (Jeremiah 6:14). Some homes are very quiet, but they are not at peace. Problems get swept under the rug, everyone pretends all is well, and the wounds fester underneath. The peace the Spirit produces does not pretend there is no conflict; it has the power to face conflict and move toward reconciliation.
47	Proverbs reminds us of the other side: “For lack of wood a fire goes out. Without gossip, a quarrel dies down” (Proverbs 26:20). Conflict is like a fire, and some words are firewood: digging up the past, sarcasm, complaining about your spouse to others. People of peace don’t run from problems; they stop adding fuel and deal with the fire while it’s still small: “What I just said was too harsh. Can we start over?”
48	One more thing must be said clearly: peace is never bought at the cost of safety. If there is physical, verbal, sexual, or financial abuse and control in the home, keeping silent to “keep the peace” is not biblical peace—it is the false peace Jeremiah described. In that case, safety comes first; seek help from a pastor and from professionals. The peace God wants is true, just, and safe.
49	How do I usually handle conflict: pretend nothing’s wrong, blow up, or talk it through gently?
50	Is there something I keep brushing off—“Forget it, it’s fine”—while my heart still hurts?
51	When something unpleasant comes up between us, would you rather we talk right away, or cool down first? How soon should we talk?
52	Could we agree on a phrase that lets each other know, “I want to talk, not fight”?
53	Today, clear up a small misunderstanding while the fire is still small
54	Write down one thing you want to talk about with your spouse but have been avoiding
55	Note down the contact details of your church’s pastor or a counseling resource
56	“Better is a dry morsel with quietness” — Lord, right now help me value peace in my family more than being right.
57	Be a Peacemaker
58	Peacemakers are willing to yield first
59	Picture two paths forking in front of you. You stop and let the other person choose first. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, today at home, I want to be the one who yields first.”
60	321 Light: Pride provokes pride—one hard word draws back a harder one; humility draws out humility—one soft word often softens the other person too. A peacemaker is someone who first becomes the “0,” self-emptied, not needing to win, seeking only oneness. This is the path where husband and wife both win.
61	When the disciples argued on the road about who was the greatest, Jesus, back in the house, didn’t let the argument go on. He sat down and said, “If any man wants to be first, he shall be last of all, and servant of all” (Mark 9:35). Jesus dealt with conflict at its root: turning a heart that grasps for greatness into a heart that serves.
62	Lord, you are the God of peace, and you call us to be peacemakers. Forgive us for the times we’ve poured fuel on the fire and the times we refused to give an inch. Give us Abram’s largeness of heart, valuing family ties above our own rights, so that between husband and wife, parents and children, and with our in-laws, we become people who yield first and bring peace. Amen.
63	Jesus said, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God” (Matthew 5:9). Notice: not “peace-lovers” but “peacemakers.” The first simply don’t want to fight; the second actively bring about reconciliation. God himself is the God who makes peace, so peacemakers look most like his children.
64	When Abram’s and Lot’s herdsmen quarreled, Abram said to Lot, “Please, let there be no strife between you and me... for we are relatives” (Genesis 13:8). He valued the relationship above the pastureland, even letting the younger Lot choose first: “If you go to the left hand, then I will go to the right. Or if you go to the right hand, then I will go to the left” (Genesis 13:9). As the elder, he could have chosen first, but he chose to yield. Peacemakers are often those willing to lay down their rights first.
65	Being a peacemaker at home isn’t only about handling conflict between husband and wife. It also means being the one who cools things down—not the one who pours fuel on the fire—among children, parents, and in-laws. Peace is not the result of one big negotiation but of daily choices: a gentle tone, listening all the way through before responding, a decision not to dig up the past, a simple “You choose first this time.”
66	In conflicts at home, am I more often the one who cools things down, or the one who adds fuel?
67	At home, is there something where I keep insisting, “I choose first; what I say goes”?
68	Think back to a time we made up after a fight. What word or action turned the atmosphere around?
69	In our families of origin or with our in-laws, is there a place where we need to be peacemakers together?
70	Today, in a small moment of friction, respond first with a gentle tone
71	Pray for a family member with whom things are tense, asking God for peace
72	Say something to your spouse that cools things down: “No rush—let’s take it slowly.”
73	“let there be no strife between you and me” — Lord, right now help me value the relationship more than winning the argument.
74	Peace Be to This House
75	Pursue peace, build one another up, and make home a house of peace
76	Quietly look around your home: the living room, the dining table, the bedroom. In your heart, bless each corner: “Peace be to this house.” Finally, hold your spouse in your heart and say, “Lord, may your peace rest on him (her).”
77	321 Light: Peace is not an outward arrangement but an inner life. It flows from the inside out: first Christ’s peace rules in my heart, then there is harmony in our marriage, and then our home can become a blessing to others. Life first, then relationships, then ministry—a home of peace is a small gospel outpost.
78	When Jesus sent out his disciples, he told them, “Into whatever house you enter, first say, ‘Peace be to this house’” (Luke 10:5). Jesus made his disciples people who carry peace into other people’s homes. When our own home has first received peace, we can bring that peace into others’ homes too.
79	God of peace, thank you for leading us to learn about peace this week. Guard our hearts and make our minds steadfast in trusting you. Help us pursue what makes for peace and what builds one another up, so that our home becomes a house of peace and a blessing to others. Peace be to this house. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.
80	Paul says, “God’s Kingdom is not eating and drinking, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit” (Romans 14:17). This verse ties together what we’ve learned these three weeks: love leads us to treat each other rightly, and peace and joy are both the work of the Spirit. When a home has these three, God’s Kingdom shows itself in that home.
81	Paul goes on: “let’s follow after things which make for peace, and things by which we may build one another up” (Romans 14:19). “Follow after” is an active verb, like a hunter on the trail of its prey. Peace doesn’t just fall into a home; it has to be pursued. In practice, that means asking with every choice: Is this word, this decision, tearing us down or building us up?
82	Isaiah says, “You will keep whoever’s mind is steadfast in perfect peace, because he trusts in you” (Isaiah 26:3). The ultimate foundation of peace is not that both spouses are mature, but that both lean on God. This week we have seen how to let Christ’s peace rule, tear down the dividing wall, refuse false peace, be peacemakers, and pursue peace together. Next week we taste the fourth flavor of the fruit: patience.
83	Lately, have my words and decisions mostly been tearing down or building up our home?
84	Is my peace built on “everything is under control,” or on a mind that steadfastly trusts God?
85	Through this week’s practice of peace, where do you think our home has changed, even a little?
86	Which family (relatives, friends, or neighbors) could we pray for together, asking God to give them peace?
87	Today, say something to your spouse that builds them up
88	Pray for a family you know: “Peace be to this house.”
89	Before bed, read Isaiah 26:3 together
90	“You will keep whoever’s mind is steadfast in perfect peace” — Lord, right now I lean on you. Keep our home in perfect peace.
