0	Fruit of the Spirit (5): Kindness
1	The Kindness Jar
2	Together, find a piggy bank or small box and name it “Our Love Deposits.”
3	Each of you write down three small things that would make you feel treated with kindness (“If you did this, I’d feel cared for”), then swap notes.
4	Agree that each day this week you’ll each do at least one thing from the other’s note, and drop a coin in the jar when you’ve done it.
5	At the end of the week, count your deposits together and pray, thanking God for the fruit of kindness he is growing in your home.
6	First Put Away, Then Be Kind
7	Kindness grows in a heart cleared of bitterness
8	Picture a flower bed overgrown with weeds. To plant new flowers, you first have to pull out the weeds. Quietly ask the Lord: “Lord, what weeds in my heart are keeping kindness from growing?”
9	321 Light: Bitterness is the fruit of the self-ruled life: my grievances, my rights that were violated. The tighter I hold on, the more bitter my heart becomes. Being self-emptied doesn’t mean pretending I wasn’t hurt; it means handing the hurt to God and not letting bitterness rule in my heart. When we let Jesus reign, kindness has room to grow.
10	Some people carried a paralyzed man on a mat to Jesus. The first thing Jesus said was, “Son, cheer up! Your sins are forgiven you” (Matthew 9:2). He didn’t begin by listing the man’s past; he used a warm word that put him at ease. Jesus’ kindness makes people feel lighter—and makes them want to be better.
11	Lord, search our hearts and put away from us all bitterness, wrath, and anger. We don’t want weeds to choke our home. Plant kindness in our hearts, so that in how we treat each other, our spouse feels lighter and accepted, just as we are before you. Amen.
12	Before Paul says “be kind,” he says, “Let all bitterness, wrath, anger, outcry, and slander be put away from you, with all malice” (Ephesians 4:31). There’s an order here: first put away, then be kind. Just as in a flower bed, if you don’t pull the weeds first, the flowers you plant will soon be choked out. Someone who harbors bitterness finds it very hard to be truly kind to their spouse.
13	These six words trace a downward path: bitterness is a sourness inside; wrath is resentment that keeps simmering; anger is the explosion; outcry is the shouting match; slander is running the other person down behind their back; malice is the intent to hurt. Many family conflicts start with just a little bitterness and slide all the way down. The sooner we deal with it at the source, the easier it is to turn back.
14	Then Paul says, “And be kind to one another, tender hearted, forgiving each other, just as God also in Christ forgave you” (Ephesians 4:32). The Greek word for “kind” carries the sense of mild, easy, pleasant to be around. When Jesus said, “my yoke is easy” (Matthew 11:30), the word “easy” comes from the same root. Kind people make those around them feel lighter, not weighed down.
15	Of the six words Paul lists, which shows up most often in my heart? What usually sets it off?
16	Am I someone who makes my spouse feel “at ease” or “weighed down”?
17	When do you feel most at ease with me? When do you feel pressure? Please say it gently.
18	Is there a “weed of bitterness” in our home that we could pray about and pull out together?
19	Write down something that has left a sour taste in your heart, and place it before the Lord
20	Today, avoid criticizing your spouse in front of others
21	Do one small thing that makes your spouse feel at ease
22	“be kind to one another, tender hearted” — Lord, right now pull the bitterness out of my heart and let me treat others with kindness.
23	God Was Kind to Me First
24	The source of kindness is the kindness God shows to the undeserving
25	Think back to what you were like before you knew the Lord, or to your least lovable moment. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, even then, you were already kind to me.”
26	321 Light: Those who have received God’s kindness find it easiest to be kind to others; those who forget the grace they received find it easiest to demand from others. The self-ruled life often assumes, “I deserved it anyway”; the self-emptied life knows, “It’s all grace.” When a life changes, relationships change.
27	At the gate of the town of Nain, Jesus saw a widow on her way to bury her only son. Scripture says, “When the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her and said to her, ‘Don’t cry’” (Luke 7:13). The widow hadn’t asked him for anything, yet Jesus stopped on his own initiative. Jesus’ kindness sees people’s needs first and reaches out first.
28	God our Savior, thank you for showing us your kindness when we least deserved it. We confess that we often forget how we received grace, and so we demand much of our spouse and give little kindness. Help us remember your mercy, and treat each other with the same kindness. Amen.
29	Paul is very frank about what we used to be: “dead through our trespasses” (Ephesians 2:5). The dead can do nothing for themselves; they can’t please God or improve themselves. This isn’t describing other people; it’s describing you and me. When God met us, we were not lovable, and we had nothing to offer in exchange.
30	Then the passage turns: “But God, being rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us” (Ephesians 2:4), made us alive together with Christ. “But God” are two of the most beautiful words in the whole Bible. Paul says God did this so “that in the ages to come he might show the exceeding riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:7). Our lives, saved by grace, are themselves a display case of God’s kindness.
31	This changes how we see our spouse. We often say, “I’ll be good to them when they’re good to me.” That is transactional kindness. God’s kindness, though, is given first and is undeserved. When I remember how I received grace, I’m more able to be kind to my spouse even when they’re not at their best or doing well. The power to be kind comes from the memory of having been treated kindly.
32	At my least lovable, how was God kind to me? Do I still remember how that moved me?
33	Has my kindness toward my spouse quietly turned into a “transaction”?
34	Was there a time when you were still kind to me even when I was at my worst? What did that mean to me?
35	When one of us is exhausted or in a bad mood, how do you most need me to be kind to you?
36	Write down a time in the past when you deeply experienced God’s kindness
37	Today, when your spouse is struggling, ask first: “How can I help you?”
38	Let go of one “I’ll be good to them when they’re good to me” thought
39	“But God, being rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us” — Lord, right now I remember your mercy, and I want to show mercy to others.
40	Leaving a Little Extra Grain
41	Kindness often hides in small, unnoticed things
42	Think of the last time someone did something small for you that warmed your heart. Quietly thank the Lord, and ask him: “What kind of extra handful of grain could I leave for my spouse today?”
43	321 Light: People matter more than tasks; relationships matter more than things. The self-ruled life keeps score in small things—“That’s not my job.” The self-emptied life sees small things as chances to love. Kindness isn’t an occasional grand gesture; it’s making our spouse feel seen and protected in everyday small things.
44	A woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years secretly touched Jesus’ clothing. When she was discovered, she fell trembling at his feet. Jesus said to her, “Daughter, cheer up. Your faith has made you well. Go in peace” (Luke 8:48). In front of everyone, Jesus restored her dignity. His kindness always cares for a person’s honor and heart.
45	Lord, thank you for the unnoticed graces you keep leaving in our lives. Give us thoughtful hearts to be kind to our spouse in everyday small things—protecting their dignity, providing for their needs, and helping them feel treasured at home. May our home be a Boaz’s field for each other. Amen.
46	Ruth was a widow and a foreigner, gleaning ears of grain in Boaz’s field to feed her mother-in-law. Boaz not only let her glean, but told his workers: “Also pull out some for her from the bundles, and leave it. Let her glean, and don’t rebuke her” (Ruth 2:16). This was quiet, thoughtful kindness: leaving a little extra grain without making her feel like a charity case.
47	Boaz did other small things too. At mealtime he invited her over: “Come here, and eat some bread, and dip your morsel in the vinegar” (Ruth 2:14). He also instructed his workers, “don’t reproach her” (Ruth 2:15). Kindness is often not a grand gesture but a thoughtful heart: protecting the other person’s dignity, providing what they need, making sure they are seen.
48	Kindness in marriage is often “leaving a little extra grain” too: remembering your spouse’s favorite flavor and picking up an extra one; seeing they’re tired and taking over the dishes; saving their face in front of family and friends; gently reminding them when they forget, instead of teasing. These small things may seem insignificant, but they are how our spouse comes to feel treasured at home.
49	Have I recently told my spouse, about some small matter, “That’s not my problem”?
50	In what small things does my spouse most need their dignity protected, or to be seen?
51	What small thing I did recently made you feel especially warm?
52	In front of family, friends, or the kids, how would you like me to “save your face”?
53	Today, pick up one little extra thing your spouse likes
54	Take over a small household chore your spouse usually does
55	Say something affirming about your spouse in front of others
56	“Let her glean, and don’t rebuke her” — Lord, right now let me be kind to my spouse with gentle words and small actions.
57	Love Deposits
58	Every small kindness is love deposited in our marriage
59	Imagine your marriage has an invisible account, and every gentle word makes a deposit. Quietly ask the Lord: “Lord, lately have I been making deposits or withdrawals?”
60	321 Light: The self-ruled life sees only how much it has deposited and calculates, “I’ve given so much—how much has my spouse paid back?” The self-emptied life makes deposits out of love, not to earn interest. We deposit because God first filled our account with grace; we are simply transferring his kindness onward.
61	On the eve of his suffering, Jesus told his disciples, “I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer” (Luke 22:15). In his final hours, he chose to share a table with them. Three years of walking and eating together were the love he deposited into his disciples’ lives, and it carried them through their darkest hours later on.
62	Lord full of kindness, thank you for filling our lives with grace. Help us write kindness and truth on the tablets of our hearts and make deposits of kindness for each other every day. When we give and see no response, keep us from losing heart. We trust that whoever waters others will be watered, and that you will bring a harvest in our home. Amen.
63	We can think of marriage as a “love account.” Every kind word, thoughtful act, and tender hug is a deposit; every sarcastic remark, forgotten promise, and harsh tone is a withdrawal. When the account is well stocked, the relationship can handle an occasional withdrawal. But if we only withdraw and never deposit, even one small comment can leave the account “overdrawn.”
64	Proverbs says, “Don’t let kindness and truth forsake you. Bind them around your neck. Write them on the tablet of your heart” (Proverbs 3:3). The Hebrew word for “kindness” here is hesed, God’s covenant love—kindness that holds on to the end. Binding it around the neck keeps it always in sight; writing it on the heart makes it part of who we are. Kindness is not an occasional mood but a habit engraved into our lives.
65	Proverbs also says, “The liberal soul shall be made fat. He who waters shall be watered also himself” (Proverbs 11:25). Sometimes we make many deposits but see no response, and we lose heart. But the heart that waters others will not dry up, and deposits of kindness are never wasted; God will bring the harvest in his time. Those closest to us need our kindness most, yet are often the ones we overlook.
66	This past week, have I made more deposits or more withdrawals in our “love account”?
67	Have I made many deposits without seeing a response, and started to lose heart or keep score?
68	For you, what feels most like a “deposit”? What feels most like a “withdrawal”?
69	For this week’s kindness jar, what kind of deposit would you most like to receive from me?
70	Today, do three small “deposit” things for your spouse
71	Avoid one “withdrawal” comment that could hurt your spouse
72	Drop a coin in the kindness jar and say why
73	“He who waters shall be watered also himself” — Lord, right now I won’t lose heart. I’ll keep making deposits of kindness for my spouse.
74	Merciful Like the Father
75	Kindness at its fullest is kindness to the ungrateful
76	Think of the Father’s face as he looks at you, his eyes full of mercy. Quietly tell him: “Father, let my eyes be like yours, looking at my spouse with mercy.”
77	321 Light: From a human point of view, my spouse doesn’t deserve it; from God’s point of view, I don’t deserve it either—yet I have received kindness. When my focus moves from my spouse’s performance to the Father’s mercy, I can treat others as he does. One purpose: to build a household that belongs to God—and the clearest mark of such a household is kindness.
78	Someone cut off the ear of the high priest’s servant—one of the men who had come to arrest Jesus. Yet Jesus “touched his ear and healed him” (Luke 22:51). In the very moment he was betrayed and seized, Jesus was still kind to those who opposed him. He shows us what the Father’s mercy looks like.
79	Merciful Father, thank you for being kind to the ungrateful, and kind to us. Make our hearts like yours, so that we are still kind to our spouse even when there is no response. Thank you for the fruit you have grown in our home these five weeks. Keep working, and fill our home with love, joy, peace, patience, and kindness, to show your glory. Amen.
80	Jesus said the Father “is kind toward the unthankful and evil” (Luke 6:35), so “be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful” (Luke 6:36). This is kindness at its highest standard: being kind not only to those who are good to me, but also to those who give nothing back. Sometimes in marriage we give, and our spouse doesn’t say thank you—or even complains. That is exactly when we learn to be like the Father.
81	Proverbs says, “The merciful man does good to his own soul, but he who is cruel troubles his own flesh” (Proverbs 11:17). Kindness isn’t only for the other person; it’s for us too. Someone who nurses bitterness and keeps score everywhere wounds their own heart first; someone who chooses kindness finds their heart set free. Kindness and forgiveness walk the same road. But we must be clear: forgiveness and kindness never mean tolerating harm. If there is violence or control in the home, safety comes first—seek help from a pastor and from professionals.
82	Over these five weeks we have tasted the first five flavors of the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, and kindness. They are one fruit, growing through the practice of 920: my spouse is my most important “2”; in Christ I practice the “0” of self-emptying; and the Spirit bears the “9”-fold fruit in our home. Four more flavors are waiting for us, so let’s keep growing together.
