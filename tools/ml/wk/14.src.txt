0	The Fruit of the Spirit (2): Joy
1	A Little Thank-You Jar
2	Together, find an empty can or glass jar and decorate it as “our family’s thank-you jar.”
3	Each write five little notes, each naming one small thing your spouse did this week that brought you joy. Fold them up and put them in the jar.
4	Take turns drawing out a note and reading it aloud. The person being thanked simply replies: “Thank you for noticing.”
5	Read Philippians 4:4 together, and agree to add a few notes to the jar every week from now on.
6	Rejoice in the Lord Always
7	The source of joy is not our circumstances but the Lord himself
8	Take a deep breath, then slowly let it out. Set down the things weighing on you today, one by one, and tell the Lord: “Lord, my joy doesn’t depend on things going well; it is found in you.”
9	321 Light: Keep the focus on Jesus, not on ourselves, not on others, not on our circumstances. Pinning our joy on our spouse is asking them to play God’s role, an unfair burden for them to carry. Let people be people, and let God be God: when my joy comes from the Lord, my spouse is set free from “the job of making me happy.”
10	On the night he was betrayed, Jesus prayed to the Father, “But now I come to you, and I say these things in the world, that they may have my joy made full in themselves” (John 17:13). The cross was right before him, yet he wanted to share his own joy with his disciples. Jesus shows us that joy wells up from our relationship with the Father, and he gladly gives that joy to us.
11	Lord, thank you for being the source of our joy. We confess that we often let our circumstances and each other’s behavior decide our mood. Today we hand all our worries to you. Give us the joy that comes from you, so that we stop demanding that each other fill us, and instead rejoice together in you. Amen.
12	When Paul wrote Philippians, he was in prison with an uncertain future. Yet in this short letter, words about joy appear again and again. He says, “Rejoice in the Lord always! Again I will say, ‘Rejoice!’” (Philippians 4:4). He says it twice, as if he knows his readers will wonder: “Can anyone really rejoice in a situation like this?” Paul’s answer is yes, because this joy is “in the Lord.”
13	Happiness and joy are not the same. Happiness is like the weather, changing with circumstances: we’re happy when we get a raise, unhappy when the kids won’t listen, and unhappy all day when our spouse forgets our anniversary. Joy is more like the deep ocean: the surface can be whipped up by wind and waves, yet the depths remain calm. The joy the Spirit produces is knowing that the Lord is with me and in control of everything, so my heart holds a steadiness and gladness that can’t be taken away.
14	Paul also shows us how to enter into this joy: “In nothing be anxious, but in everything, by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God” (Philippians 4:6). What most often steals a couple’s joy is not some great disaster, but accumulated worries: bills, work, children, parents. When we bring our worries to God together, joy has room to grow.
15	Lately, has my joy been more like the “weather” or the “deep ocean”? What changes my mood most easily?
16	Have I, without realizing it, placed the responsibility of “making me happy” on my spouse?
17	What worries have been weighing on your heart lately? How can we hand them to God together today?
18	When do you think our home feels most joyful? What are we usually doing at those times?
19	Write down one worry today, and pray it over with your spouse, entrusting it to God
20	Tell your spouse: “You don’t have to be responsible for making me happy. I’m doing well in the Lord.”
21	Find a moment today to quietly recite Philippians 4:4
22	“Rejoice in the Lord always” — Lord, right now, whatever my situation, I choose to rejoice in you.
23	Even Though the Vines Bear No Fruit
24	Joy doesn’t depend on the harvest, but on the God who saves me
25	Picture a parched field: no fruit on the trees, no sheep in the fold. In the middle of that field, someone lifts their head and sings. Quietly ask yourself: “Where in my field is there still no harvest?”
26	321 Light: From a human point of view, no harvest means no reason for joy; from God’s point of view, God himself is the reason. As we turn from earth to heaven and from the temporary to the eternal, joy is no longer tied to the harvest. This isn’t passive resignation; it is actively letting Jesus reign.
27	After the Last Supper, “When they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives” (Matthew 26:30). Gethsemane and the cross lay just ahead, yet he sang with his disciples. Jesus’ joy did not depend on his circumstances; it was built on trust in the Father’s will.
28	God who saves us, you see the trees in our home that have not yet borne fruit, and you see the disappointment in our hearts. We won’t pretend it doesn’t hurt, but we choose to look to you. Be our strength, steady our feet on the high places, and after every “even though,” help us say “yet” together. Amen.
29	The prophet Habakkuk wrote one of the bravest songs in the Bible: “For even though the fig tree doesn’t flourish, nor fruit be in the vines... yet I will rejoice in the LORD. I will be joyful in the God of my salvation!” (Habakkuk 3:17–18). In an agricultural society, these words amount to saying: my paycheck is gone, my savings are gone, every security I had is gone. And after all those “even though”s, he speaks one “yet.”
30	Every couple goes through seasons when “the vines bear no fruit”: perhaps long-term financial pressure, perhaps a child longed for who still hasn’t come, perhaps a parent’s illness, a setback at work, or a low point in the marriage itself. The Bible doesn’t ask us to pretend these don’t hurt. Habakkuk’s joy is not denial of reality; it is choosing, after seeing reality clearly, to turn our eyes to God anyway.
31	His reason is this: “GOD, the Lord, is my strength. He makes my feet like deer’s feet, and enables me to go in high places” (Habakkuk 3:19). A deer on a steep mountain path doesn’t rely on the path being smooth, but on the sureness of its feet. Joy doesn’t flatten the mountain path, but it does steady our feet. When a husband and wife say “yet” together in hard times, they are helping each other stand firm.
32	What “vine” in our home still bears no fruit? What do I honestly feel about it deep down?
33	After that “even though,” can I honestly speak a “yet” to God?
34	What is the biggest “even though” in our home right now? Let’s finish this sentence together: “Even though…, yet we will rejoice in God, because…”
35	In hard seasons, how would you most like me to be with you, instead of rushing to cheer you up?
36	Write your family’s own “Even though… yet…” sentence
37	Today, don’t rush to comfort your spouse; first listen until they’ve said everything on their heart
38	Set Habakkuk 3:18 as your phone wallpaper
39	“yet I will rejoice in the LORD. I will be joyful in the God of my salvation!” — Lord, right now, even when things aren’t going my way, I still rejoice in you.
40	A Home That Gives Thanks in Everything
41	Thanksgiving is the soil of joy and the culture of a home
42	Be still for a moment and count three small things from today that you’re thankful for, plus one more that has to do with your spouse. Slowly tell the Lord: “Lord, thank you. All of these are your gifts.”
43	321 Light: Complaining is the language of the self-ruled life, because the focus is on “what I didn’t get”; thanksgiving is the language of the self-emptied life, because the focus is on “what God has already given.” Change your mindset and your life will change: when I shift from “they should have done it” to “thank you for doing it,” the air in our home changes.
44	At Lazarus’ tomb, before the dead man had come out, Jesus lifted up his eyes and said, “Father, I thank you that you listened to me” (John 11:41). He gave thanks before he saw the result. Jesus shows us that thanksgiving isn’t something we say only once everything is fine; in trust, we speak it first.
45	Gracious Lord, we often forget your benefits, and we often take our spouse’s efforts for granted. Open our eyes to see your grace every day. Help us say more thanks and fewer complaints at home, so that thanksgiving becomes our family’s culture and joy becomes the air in our home. Amen.
46	Paul puts three things together: “Always rejoice. Pray without ceasing. In everything give thanks” (1 Thessalonians 5:16–18). Joy, prayer, and thanksgiving are like three strands woven together. Thanksgiving is especially like soil: the same seed won’t grow in hard ground, but sprouts in soft, loose earth. In a home where people often say “thank you,” joy grows easily.
47	David tells himself, “Praise the LORD, my soul, and don’t forget all his benefits” (Psalm 103:2). He has to remind himself, because people forget so easily. Marriage is the same: when we were newly married, we were touched when our spouse poured us a glass of water; ten years later, they pour us water every day and we take it for granted. Taking things for granted is the biggest thief of gratitude.
48	Thanksgiving can become a family culture. Culture isn’t built by one moving moment, but by repeated habits: a prayer of thanks before meals, naming one thing you’re thankful for in your spouse before bed, and saying it out loud, specifically, when a child or family member does something good. Notice that Paul says “In everything give thanks,” not “give thanks for everything.” We don’t give thanks for bad things, but in anything at all, we can still find God’s grace.
49	Of the things my spouse does for me, which one have I long taken for granted and not said thank you for in a long time?
50	In the words I’ve spoken lately, have there been more complaints or more thanks?
51	Each name three things from this week you want to thank the other for. The more specific, the better.
52	What small habit of thanksgiving could our family build, so that it happens every day or every week?
53	Say a specific “Thank you for…” to your spouse three times today
54	When you pray before a meal, add a sentence thanking God for your spouse
55	Before bed, each share the one thing you are most thankful for today
56	“In everything give thanks” — Lord, right now I give you thanks for one small thing.
57	Bringing Joy Home
58	The joy of the Lord is the strength of the whole family
59	Think about the moment you arrive at your front door each day. What expression do you carry in with you? Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, today let me bring your joy through the door, not my exhaustion.”
60	321 Light: Live from the heart and you find joy; live from the head and you fret. Come home with a head full of work and calculations, and home becomes just another office; hand your heart to the Holy Spirit, and joy will flow out. People matter more than tasks: don’t let the day’s tasks crush the people in your home.
61	Passing through Jericho, Jesus looked up at Zacchaeus in the tree and said, “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house,” and Zacchaeus “hurried, came down, and received him joyfully” (Luke 19:5–6). When Jesus walked into that home, it was filled with joy and change. When we let Jesus into our own hearts first, we will carry his joy home with us.
62	Lord, forgive us for so often bringing our bad moods home and making the people closest to us bear our exhaustion. Let the joy of the Lord be our strength, so that we walk through the door with smiles and blessings, and our home becomes good medicine for each other rather than another battlefield. Amen.
63	Proverbs says, “A glad heart makes a cheerful face, but an aching heart breaks the spirit” (Proverbs 15:13). The atmosphere in a home is a lot like air conditioning: when one person walks in angry, the whole house cools down. Many people are all smiles with clients at work, but at home they dump all their exhaustion and bad moods on the people closest to them. We give our best to strangers and save our worst for our family.
64	Joy flows from the heart to the face. This isn’t a call to put on a fake smile; it’s a reminder that one person’s heart affects the whole dinner table. Proverbs also says, “one who has a cheerful heart enjoys a continual feast” (Proverbs 15:15). The same plain rice and vegetables are a feast to a joyful heart; the finest delicacies taste of nothing to a bitter one.
65	When the people heard God’s law and remembered their failures, they wept. But Nehemiah told them, “Don’t be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength” (Nehemiah 8:10). Joy is not just a mood; it is strength. Couples can practice a “doorway prayer”: with your hand on the doorknob, take a breath, say one sentence to the Lord, and then open the door and walk in. Those few seconds can turn the day’s battlefield into the rest of home.
66	Looking back over this week, what expression and mood did I most often carry through the front door?
67	Have I, without realizing it, been taking out the pressures of the outside world on my spouse?
68	When I get home each day (or when you do), how would you like us to greet each other?
69	What small things best help you laugh again after an exhausting day?
70	Before you open the door when you get home today, pray a “doorway prayer”
71	As soon as you walk in, give your spouse a smile or a hug before saying anything else
72	At dinner, share one thing that made you laugh today
73	“the joy of the LORD is your strength” — Lord, right now renew my heart, and let me bring joy home.
74	A Couple Who Weep and Rejoice Together
75	True joy is not never crying, but walking together until morning comes
76	Be still and consider what has been going on in your spouse’s heart lately: are they rejoicing, or are they grieving? Tell the Lord: “Lord, help my heart keep pace with theirs.”
77	321 Light: The self-ruled only want others to match their own emotions: when I’m happy, you should laugh with me; when I’m sad, you should understand me. The self-emptied first set aside their own channel and tune in to their spouse’s. When two are of one heart, joy is doubled and sorrow is halved. This is the beauty of God’s design in making husband and wife one.
78	Jesus knew he was about to raise Lazarus, yet when he saw Mary weeping, “he groaned in the spirit and was troubled” (John 11:33), and “Jesus wept” (John 11:35). He didn’t rush to say, “Don’t cry, it’ll be fine in a minute.” Jesus is our example: first weep with those who weep, then bring the joy of resurrection.
79	Lord, thank you for being with us in our nights of weeping, and for promising that joy comes in the morning. Give us soft hearts, able to rejoice with our spouse without envy, and to weep with our spouse without impatience. Turn our family’s mourning into dancing, so that our hearts sing praise to you forever. Amen.
80	Picture a couple: on the day the husband is promoted, the wife feels a twinge of envy; on another day, when the wife is sad, the husband rushes to say, “Don’t overthink it.” Rejoicing together requires self-emptying, not envy; weeping together also requires self-emptying, not rushing to fix the other’s feelings. Since husband and wife are one, when one rejoices, the other rejoices too; when one suffers, the other shares the load.
81	The fruit of joy doesn’t mean we must smile all the time. The psalmist says, “Weeping may stay for the night, but joy comes in the morning” (Psalm 30:5). Scripture admits there is weeping in the night; it only promises the night won’t last forever. The nights a couple walks through together might be a job loss, a miscarriage, the long wait of infertility, the death of a loved one, or a crisis in the marriage. Godly joy doesn’t skip these nights; it holds hands through the night and waits for morning together.
82	Jesus told his grieving disciples, “you now have sorrow, but I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice, and no one will take your joy away from you” (John 16:22). This week we have seen that the source of joy is the Lord; that joy doesn’t depend on the harvest; that thanksgiving is the soil of joy; that joy can be brought home; and that joy at its deepest is two people weeping and rejoicing together in the Lord. Next week we’ll taste the third flavor: peace.
83	When my spouse has good news, can I truly rejoice for them? Or is there comparison or envy in my heart?
84	When my spouse is sad, do I more often weep with them, or rush to give answers and want them to feel better quickly?
85	Think back to a “night” we walked through together. What made you feel you weren’t alone then?
86	Of this week’s practices of joy, which habit would you most like us to keep?
87	Ask your spouse today: “Lately, does your heart feel more like weeping or laughing?”
88	Celebrate a recent good thing in your spouse’s life in a specific way
89	Read Psalm 30:5 together, and pray for friends or family going through a dark night
90	“Weeping may stay for the night, but joy comes in the morning” — Lord, right now I hand you my tears in the night, and I wait for your morning.
