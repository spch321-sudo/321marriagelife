0	Denying Ourselves: Taking Up the Cross to Follow the Lord
1	A Day Planned for You
2	Ahead of time, each think of one activity “my spouse loves but I’m usually less keen on.” Do it together this weekend, with no complaining and no checking the clock.
3	Swap one household chore the other usually handles. Do it with care, without seeking credit or reminding your spouse what you did.
4	In the evening, read Luke 9:23 together and take turns saying: “This week, one way I saw you deny yourself for me was... Thank you.”
5	Lay a hand on each other’s shoulder and pray: “Lord, help us follow you every day, and give ourselves gladly for each other.”
6	Denying Ourselves: The First Step in Following
7	Denying ourselves isn’t losing ourselves; it’s finding life in Jesus
8	Imagine Jesus walking ahead of you, turning back to say, “Come, follow me.” Be still for a moment and ask yourself: “Today, what am I clutching tightly and unwilling to let go of?” Bring it before him.
9	321 Light: We deny ourselves for Jesus and for others, not for ourselves. The self-ruled heart asks, “What’s in it for me?” The one who denies self asks, “Will this let Jesus reign?” Only when we stop making ourselves the center do we truly live as the people God originally made us to be.
10	In the garden of Gethsemane, Jesus was so sorrowful he was close to death. He honestly poured out his struggle to the Father, yet in the end he said: “not my will, but yours, be done” (Luke 22:42). Jesus shows us that self-denial isn’t pretending we have no feelings; it is bringing our real feelings and still choosing to obey the Father.
11	Lord Jesus, you said that anyone who would follow you must deny himself and take up his cross every day. We admit that we often just want to live our own way. Give us strength to lay ourselves down in the small things of each day, not to get something in return, but because we love you. In the places where we let go of ourselves, let us receive the richer life you give. Amen.
12	Jesus said: “If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me” (Luke 9:23). There are three actions here: deny yourself, take up your cross, and follow. Denying ourselves doesn’t mean hating ourselves or having no self; it means saying no to “I’m in charge.” It’s like handing the steering wheel to someone who knows the road better: I’m still in the car, but I’m no longer the one deciding where it goes.
13	Many English translations add one more word here: take up your cross “daily.” (The WEB text leaves that word out, but the call is plainly to an everyday way of life.) Self-denial isn’t one grand, heroic sacrifice; it’s small choices every day. In marriage, the cross is rarely dramatic. Most often it looks like listening to the same worry one more time, giving up the remote one more time, being the first to back down one more time. These small moments of “not my way” are the cross we take up each day.
14	Jesus went on: “For whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever will lose his life for my sake will save it” (Luke 9:24). The key words are “for my sake.” We don’t deny ourselves to earn something back from our spouse, but for Jesus. People who cling tightly to their own rights often lose the joy of marriage; those willing to lay themselves down for the Lord and for their spouse find a richer life instead. In the end, denying ourselves isn’t a loss; it’s a gain.
15	At home today, what is the one thing I most want to have “my way”?
16	Do I often treat self-denial as a deal: I gave in to you, so now you should give in to me?
17	In our marriage, what “small cross” do you quietly carry every day? I want to understand better how hard it is for you.
18	How can we remind each other to choose “not my way” in small things, without making the other feel pressured?
19	In one small matter, choose “not my way”
20	Don’t remind your spouse of what you gave up for him/her
21	When you get up in the morning, say: “Lord, today I will follow you.”
22	“let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me” — Lord Jesus, right now I lay down my own way and follow you.
23	Not Pleasing Ourselves
24	Giving way on preferences and habits is love in its most everyday form
25	Think about all your preferences: the flavors you like, the room temperature, your daily rhythm, the pace at which you talk. None of these are sins; they’re all gifts from God. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, teach me to put my preferences behind love.”
26	321 Light: Treating my preferences as the standard is the most everyday form of self on the throne. People matter more than tasks; relationships matter more than things. Don’t break the heart of the one beside you over the thermostat setting. When we don’t seek to please ourselves, our home takes on the fragrance of Christ.
27	Jesus sat by the well, tired and thirsty. When the disciples came back with food and urged him to eat, he said: “My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work” (John 4:34). It wasn’t that Jesus didn’t care about his own needs, but that something satisfied him more than satisfying himself: doing the Father’s will and caring for the person in front of him.
28	Lord, thank you for giving us different personalities and preferences. We admit that we often treat our own preferences as the standard and argue over small things. Help us not to please ourselves, but to learn to please and build up each other. In the small everyday ways we give way, may we each feel valued, and may our home carry the fragrance of Christ. Amen.
29	The most common friction in marriage usually isn’t over matters of right and wrong, but over preferences: what to set the thermostat to, what to have for dinner, whether to go out or stay home on the weekend, which end to squeeze the toothpaste from. None of these have a right answer, yet they’re exactly where two people most easily dig in. That’s because deep down we assume that my preferences are “normal” and yours need correcting. Over time, love gets worn thin by one small tug-of-war after another.
30	Paul says: “Now we who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak, and not to please ourselves” (Romans 15:1). He was originally talking about believers’ differing views on food and holy days: in matters that aren’t about right and wrong, let love decide. Then he says: “Let each one of us please his neighbor for that which is good, to be building him up” (Romans 15:2). At home, our nearest neighbor is our spouse.
31	There’s just one reason: “For even Christ didn’t please himself” (Romans 15:3). This doesn’t mean we must suppress our preferences forever; it means letting our preferences step back behind love. Sometimes I give way to you, sometimes you give way to me, sometimes we find a third way together. What matters is that neither of us treats “I like it this way” as the highest law in the house. Denying ourselves in our preferences is a small thing, yet it tells our spouse most clearly: “You matter more than my habits.”
32	In our home, which “I like it this way” have I unconsciously turned into a house rule?
33	The last time I gave way to my spouse’s preference, did I do it gladly or resentfully?
34	Name one small thing you really love that you feel I don’t quite understand, and help me see what it means to you.
35	In what everyday matter could we try “doing it your way this time”?
36	Choose one small thing and do it the way your spouse prefers
37	Ask your spouse: “How would you like to spend this evening?”
38	Refrain from commenting on one of your spouse’s habits that differs from yours
39	“even Christ didn’t please himself” — Lord, right now let me put my preferences behind love.
40	As Working for the Lord
41	Sharing the load of chores and time is a practice of self-denial
42	Think of what someone has already done in your home today: a meal cooked, laundry washed, bills paid, rides given. Quietly tell the Lord: “Thank you for all the unseen giving in our home.”
43	321 Light: Running a home by keeping score only breeds more resentment the more we count; running a home by sharing the load grows sweeter the more we do. The focus isn’t on how much my spouse has done, but on what Jesus sees. People matter more than tasks: housework is meant to give the family rest, not to prove who works harder.
44	The risen Jesus lit a charcoal fire on the shore, cooked fish and bread, and said to the disciples who had toiled all night: “Come and eat breakfast!” (John 21:12). He is the Lord of glory, yet he made breakfast with his own hands for people who were exhausted. Jesus shows us that the greatest love is often hidden in the most ordinary chores.
45	Lord Jesus, you are the Lord of glory, yet you were willing to make breakfast for your disciples. Forgive our hearts that keep score over chores and count up our time. Help us see the burdens on each other’s shoulders and reach out to share them; and let us do every chore as for you, so that ordinary days become worship offered to you. Amen.
46	Someone once said housework is like a treadmill that never stops: the dishes you wash today become a full sink tomorrow; the clothes you fold today become a pile again the day after. Many couples’ resentment builds up on that treadmill as they silently tally, “I do more than you.” Over time, both feel like they’re the one getting the short end of the stick. Housework itself isn’t the problem; the problem is that we’ve replaced sharing the load with keeping score.
47	Paul says: “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2). Bearing one another’s burdens isn’t splitting everything fifty-fifty; it’s noticing the weight on the other’s shoulders and reaching out first. In some seasons one spouse is especially busy at work; in others one is especially worn out from caring for children or aging parents. A self-denying view of housework doesn’t ask, “Is this my job?” but “What’s your heaviest burden today?” Time is the same: giving up some of my own rest is often the most practical way to share the load.
48	The deepest change comes when the One we work for changes: “And whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men” (Colossians 3:23). When I wash the dishes for the Lord, I don’t need my spouse’s praise to keep me going; when I take out the trash for the Lord, ordinary chores become worship. This doesn’t mean one spouse can push everything onto the other; it means both of us treat housework as a gift offered to the Lord.
49	Do I keep a “chore ledger” in my heart? What was I tallying the last time I kept score?
50	If I did one of tomorrow’s chores “as for the Lord,” how would my attitude be different?
51	In this season, what’s the heaviest burden on your shoulders (chores, work, caring for family, or something else)? How can I help carry it?
52	Is there a chore in our home that we could redistribute or do together, to make things a little lighter for both of us?
53	Take over one chore your spouse usually handles
54	Tell your spouse: “Thank you for all the things you do that I don’t see.”
55	Give your spouse thirty minutes to rest properly
56	“whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord” — Lord, right now the work in my hands is an offering to you.
57	A Grain of Wheat Falls into the Earth
58	Self-denial is like a seed falling into the ground: only by dying can it bear much fruit
59	Imagine holding a single grain of wheat in your palm. As long as you hold it, it will only ever be one grain; only when it goes into the soil can it grow into a field of wheat. Tell the Lord: “Lord, I’m willing to place myself in your hands and let you make me fruitful.”
60	321 Light: The self-ruled heart tries to preserve itself and remains a single grain; the self-emptied heart is willing to fall into the ground and leaves the fruit to God. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: when my life first falls into the ground at home, God makes our relationship fruitful, and then from our home bears fruit that blesses others.
61	Jesus and his disciples were so busy they had no time even to eat. They went off hoping to rest in a deserted place, but the crowds got there first. “Jesus came out, saw a great multitude, and he had compassion on them” (Mark 6:34). He set aside his own rest and began to teach them. Jesus shows us that giving up our time flows from a heart of compassion.
62	Lord Jesus, you are the grain of wheat that fell into the ground and died, bearing the fruit of life for us. We admit that we often want to protect our time, our plans, and our pride. Give us courage to place ourselves in your hands and to sow seed gladly in our marriage. And when we can’t yet see the fruit, help us trust the season you have set. Amen.
63	Jesus said: “unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit” (John 12:24). This is the most beautiful picture of self-denial. A grain of wheat kept in a pretty box can last a long time, but it will always be alone. When it falls into the ground and its husk splits open, it seems to lose itself, but in fact it is becoming much more.
64	Self-denial in marriage is like that. Setting aside your weekend plans for your spouse seems like a “loss”; spending your after-work time really listening to your spouse seems like a “waste”; adjusting your pace when an opportunity comes along, for the sake of your family’s needs, seems like “getting the short end.” But the time and energy buried in the soil slowly grow into trust, intimacy, and security. Self-denial is never simply losing something; it is sowing seed.
65	Jesus went on: “If anyone serves me, let him follow me. Where I am, there my servant will also be” (John 12:26). Jesus himself is that grain of wheat; his death bore the fruit of countless saved lives. To follow him is to fall into the ground with him and bear fruit with him. Seeds need time, and fruit doesn’t show up the same day; self-denial in marriage also means waiting for God’s season.
66	What am I gripping tightly right now, unwilling to let it “fall into the ground”: my time, my plans, or my pride?
67	Because I haven’t seen any fruit yet, do I doubt that my self-denial is doing any good?
68	Looking back over our marriage, when did something you gave up later bear good fruit? I want to thank you.
69	This week, what time could each of us “plant” in the other?
70	Take a block of your own time and spend it fully with your spouse
71	Write down one way you’re willing to “plant” yourself for your family
72	Thank your spouse for something he/she once gave up for the family
73	“But if it dies, it bears much fruit” — Lord, right now I’m willing to let go of myself. Make me fruitful.
74	Following Gladly, Not Grudgingly
75	Self-denial flows from the love of Christ: a glad choice, not a forced endurance
76	Think back over the things you’ve given up for your spouse this week. Is your heart light right now, or heavy? You don’t need to hide it. Tell the Lord honestly how you feel, and ask him to move you afresh with his love.
77	321 Light: Resentment means the “self” is still there, just stepping back for a while and waiting to collect what it’s owed; self-denial means the “self” has been handed over to the Lord, so there’s nothing to collect. When we move from the human point of view to God’s, from the temporary to the eternal, self-denial turns from a chore into the joy of following the Lord.
78	Jesus said: “No one takes it away from me, but I lay it down by myself” (John 10:18). His self-denial wasn’t helpless surrender but love with authority. When his time had not yet come, he avoided danger; when his time came, he walked to the cross willingly. Jesus shows us that self-denial is a clear-eyed, free choice made out of love.
79	Lord Jesus, for the joy set before you, you endured the cross, and your love compels us. Heal the bitterness that has built up in our hearts from resentment, so that our self-denial flows from love rather than from suppression. Keep everyone in our home safe, and give us wisdom to discern and courage to seek help. Let us no longer live for ourselves, but for you. Amen.
80	Self-denial and resentment can look alike on the outside, but inside they’re completely different. The resentful person says, “Fine, whatever. It’s no use saying anything anyway.” They keep a ledger in their heart, and the more they give in, the more bitter they become. The self-denying person says, “I want to, because I love the Lord and I love you.” There’s no ledger in their heart, and the more they give, the bigger their heart grows. The difference is what drives them: “For the love of Christ compels us” (2 Corinthians 5:14). Not forced, but moved by love.
81	Jesus endured the cross “for the joy that was set before him” (Hebrews 12:2). He saw not only the suffering in front of him but also the joy ahead. Those who deny themselves also have joy in view: a self more like Christ, a home more united, a reward in eternity. From a temporary point of view, self-denial is a loss; from an eternal point of view, it’s an investment. Christ died for all “that those who live should no longer live to themselves” (2 Corinthians 5:15).
82	We must also say clearly: self-denial is not enduring abuse. Jesus’ self-denial was a clear-eyed choice. When people tried to throw him off a cliff, “he, passing through the middle of them, went his way” (Luke 4:30). Physical, verbal, sexual, and financial abuse and control are not a cross to bear, and they are not submission; forgiveness does not mean staying in danger. If you are in a situation like this, your safety comes first. Please seek help from a pastor and from professionals as soon as possible. God wants willing love, not wounded silence.
