0	Fruit of the Spirit (8): Gentleness
1	An Evening with the Volume Turned Down
2	Choose a quiet evening. Turn off the TV and phones, light a small lamp, and make a pot of warm tea.
3	All evening, deliberately speak a little more softly and slowly than usual, and notice how the atmosphere at home changes.
4	Take turns finishing this sentence: “The time you treated me with gentleness that I’ll never forget was...”
5	Read Proverbs 15:1 together, then pray a blessing over each other’s words: “Lord, make our tongues a tree of life.”
6	Gentleness Is Not Weakness
7	Gentleness is strength tamed and governed by God
8	Make a tight fist and feel its strength, then slowly open your hand and relax it. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, I have strength, and I have a temper. Today I want to place them in your hands and let you govern them.”
9	321 Light: Proud strength wants to prove, “I’m right, I’m stronger than you”—it grabs for power and wants to be king. Gentleness hands strength back to God and returns to our rightful place: let people be people, and let God be God. When husband and wife are both willing to be governed by God, home is no longer a wrestling ring.
10	When Jesus was arrested, Peter drew his sword to defend him, but Jesus said, “Or do you think that I couldn’t ask my Father, and he would even now send me more than twelve legions of angels?” (Matthew 26:53). He had unmatched power, yet chose not to use it to protect himself. Jesus’ gentleness was not powerlessness; it was mighty power governed by the Father’s will.
11	Lord, you are the Almighty God, yet you treat us gently. Govern our strength, our tempers, and our tongues, so that we don’t use our strength to overpower each other but to serve each other. Make us gentle people, with clear convictions and soft hearts, showing your character in our home. Amen.
12	A fine horse runs fast and is far stronger than any person, yet once it has been tamed, a light pull on the reins from its rider brings it to a stop. Its strength hasn’t disappeared; it is simply under control. That is exactly what the Bible means by gentleness: not the absence of strength, but strength governed by God. Jesus said, “Blessed are the gentle, for they shall inherit the earth” (Matthew 5:5).
13	Numbers says, “Now the man Moses was very humble, more than all the men who were on the surface of the earth” (Numbers 12:3). The word translated “humble” here also carries the sense of meekness and gentleness. Moses was no weakling: he dared to face Pharaoh and led Israel out of Egypt. But when his own sister and brother spoke against him, he didn’t rush to hit back; he left the matter with God. That is gentleness: having the power to strike back, and choosing not to use it.
14	At home, it’s easy to misunderstand gentleness, thinking it means never speaking up, having no opinions, letting others push you around. In fact, gentle people can hold clear convictions and can say “no”; they just don’t use a sharp tone or try to crush the other person. Gentleness is not a quiet voice but a heart governed by God; it isn’t having no boundaries, but still loving within those boundaries.
15	Have I ever mistaken gentleness for weakness, and so been unwilling to be gentle?
16	At home, has my strength (my way with words, my emotions, my finances, my physical strength) been used to overpower my spouse?
17	When do you think I’m most gentle? When am I least gentle? Please say it gently.
18	Is there a topic in our home that often turns into a “wrestling match”? How could we talk about it differently?
19	Today, before you speak, say in your heart: “Lord, please govern me.”
20	Identify one situation in which you habitually speak in a forceful tone
21	Say one gentle word of affirmation to your spouse
22	“Blessed are the gentle” — Lord, right now govern my strength and my temper.
23	A Gentle Answer Turns Away Wrath
24	One gentle word can put out a fire
25	Recall the moment the last argument broke out at home. Which words set the fire going? Don’t blame anyone; just gently ask the Lord: “Lord, next time, let me be the one who pours on water.”
26	321 Light: Living from the head by ourselves, our first reaction is to justify and hit back, and the more we talk, the more upset we get. Living from the heart by the Spirit, we can pause for a second before we speak and ask, “Lord, am I about to pour water or oil?” Pride provokes pride, but gentleness draws out gentleness.
27	During his trial, an officer struck Jesus. He neither cursed nor shrank back in silence, but calmly said, “If I have spoken evil, testify of the evil; but if well, why do you beat me?” (John 18:23). Jesus shows us that gentleness doesn’t mean not telling the truth; it means speaking calmly and clearly even under provocation.
28	Lord, keep us from harsh words and give us gentle answers. When the other is angry, help us pour on water instead of oil; when we want to defend ourselves, help us listen first and pause first. May our tongues be a tree of life in our home, bringing each other healing and rest. Amen.
29	When a kitchen fire breaks out, some people pour on water and some pour on oil, with completely different results. Proverbs says, “A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger” (Proverbs 15:1). When spouses argue, every reply is really a choice: water or oil? If my spouse is already angry and I answer even louder, I’m pouring oil on the fire.
30	A gentle answer isn’t lying or pretending everything’s fine; it’s telling the truth in a way the other person can receive. Say your spouse complains, “You forgot again!” A harsh answer is, “Like you never forget anything!” A gentle answer is, “I’m sorry, I really did forget. I know it matters to you. Can I make it up now?” The situation itself hasn’t changed, but the atmosphere is completely different.
31	Proverbs goes on: “A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but deceit in it crushes the spirit” (Proverbs 15:4). Our tongues can plant a tree of life at home, where others can rest in its shade—or become a knife that crushes the spirit. Our most gentle moments are often not when we’re in a good mood, but when our spouse is upset and we choose to catch them with gentleness.
32	In an argument, what’s the line I most often use to “pour on oil”?
33	When my spouse is upset, how do I usually respond?
34	When I speak, which tone hurts you most easily? Which tone makes you feel most “caught” and held?
35	Let’s come up with one “gentle answer” together that we can open with the next time there’s conflict.
36	Today, pause for one second before responding to your spouse
37	Rewrite one sharp thing you often say into a gentle version
38	In a gentle voice, say thank you to someone in your family
39	“A gentle answer turns away wrath” — Lord, right now let me speak words that put out anger.
40	Learning Gentleness from Jesus
41	Take his yoke and learn his gentle and humble ways
42	Imagine Jesus sitting right beside you. There is no blame in his eyes, only gentleness. Quietly listen as he says to you: “Come to me, and learn from me.” Place today’s weariness and impatience under his gentle gaze.
43	321 Light: Jesus is my example. In him, gentleness and humility belong together: those who are humble in heart are gentle with others; those who are proud in heart naturally speak sharply. To bear the fruit of gentleness, our roots must go down into self-emptied humility.
44	When Jesus entered Jerusalem, the people were waiting for a mighty king, yet he fulfilled the words of Scripture: “behold, your King comes to you, humble, and riding on a donkey” (Matthew 21:5). The King of kings chose a donkey, not a warhorse. Jesus shows us that true authority reveals itself through humility and gentleness.
45	Gentle and humble Lord Jesus, thank you that you never break a bruised reed or snuff out a smoldering wick. We want to take your yoke and learn from you. Change what is impatient and sharp in us, so that when the other is at their weakest, we lift them up with your gentleness and make our home a place of rest. Amen.
46	In the Gospels, Jesus rarely describes his own inner life, but once he said, “Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29). A yoke is the wooden frame placed on the necks of two oxen pulling a plow together. In those days, an experienced older ox was often yoked with a young one. Jesus invites us to share his yoke, and step by step learn his gentleness.
47	Gentleness is not an inborn personality trait but a life learned by following Jesus. Some of us are impatient by nature, some of us are blunt talkers; neither is an excuse, and neither is a reason to despair. Like a young ox yoked to an older one, we may lurch around at first, but gradually our steps fall into rhythm. When husband and wife are both under Jesus’ yoke, they become more and more like him, and more and more in step with each other.
48	Matthew quotes Isaiah to describe Jesus: “He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax” (Matthew 12:20)—a lamp wick that is barely smoldering. When our spouse is weak, weary, or has failed, they are like a bruised reed or a smoldering wick. At that moment, one criticism might snap them, one sigh might snuff them out. Learning Jesus’ gentleness means carefully lifting them up and gently shielding them when they are at their most fragile.
49	Is my spouse like a “bruised reed” in some area right now? How have I been treating them?
50	What impatience or sharpness have I written off as “just my personality,” unwilling to let Jesus change it?
51	In what area lately have you felt like a “smoldering wick,” needing me to be especially gentle with you?
52	Where do you think the two of us are most out of step? How could we keep pace with Jesus together?
53	Today, choose not to criticize one of your spouse’s weaknesses
54	Say something protective, such as: “Take your time—it’s okay.”
55	Write Matthew 11:29 on a sticky note and put it where you’ll see it often
56	“I am gentle and humble in heart” — Lord Jesus, right now I want to learn your gentle and humble ways.
57	Restoring with a Gentle Spirit
58	When our spouse falls, gentleness restores rather than condemns
59	Think of a time when you yourself fell or did something wrong. How did you most want others to treat you? Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, teach me to treat my spouse the way you have gently treated me.”
60	321 Light: When the self-ruled life sees a spouse do wrong, what wells up is, “See? I told you so!”—using the other’s failure to lift ourselves up. The self-emptied life sees, “They need help, and I could fall too.” The focus is not on condemning but on restoring; people matter more than issues.
61	When the disciples were weak, Jesus often lifted them up first, then taught them. When Peter began to sink in the water, “Immediately Jesus stretched out his hand, took hold of him, and said to him, ‘You of little faith, why did you doubt?’” (Matthew 14:31). Jesus took hold of him first, then spoke; his correction always came after his gentle hand.
62	Lord, you never look down on our weakness, and you always gently restore us. Forgive us for rushing to condemn and to prove ourselves right when our spouse falls. Give us gentle hearts and wise words, to lift up first and speak second. And protect everyone in our home, so that our home becomes a safe place where things can be mended. Amen.
63	When a bone is dislocated, a good doctor doesn’t yank it hard, but uses a precise, gentle technique to ease it back into place. Paul says, “Brothers, even if a man is caught in some fault, you who are spiritual must restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness” (Galatians 6:1). The word “restore” originally meant to mend or put back in place—like a doctor setting a bone, or a fisherman mending a net.
64	When our spouse does wrong, our most common reaction is to point out the mistake, prove we’re right, and demand an admission. We may be right on every point, but it’s like yanking a dislocated bone: the other person only hurts more and wants to escape. Paul adds a reminder: “looking to yourself so that you also aren’t tempted” (Galatians 6:1). Gentle people remember that they too could fall.
65	We need to say this clearly: restoring with gentleness applies to ordinary weaknesses and faults, not to putting up with harm. Physical, verbal, sexual, or financial abuse and control cannot be fixed by gentleness, and enduring them is never what submission requires. In that situation, safety comes first; forgiveness does not mean staying in danger. Please seek help from a pastor and from professionals right away. Gentleness also includes protecting yourself and your family.
66	When my spouse does wrong, is my first reaction more like “a doctor setting a bone” or “a judge passing sentence”?
67	Have I ever used my spouse’s failure to prove that I’m better?
68	When you’ve done something wrong, what would you most like me to say or do first?
69	Think back to a time I stood by you when you were weak. What made you feel treated gently? If your spouse can’t join you right now, write down what you’d like to say to them.
70	When your spouse makes a small mistake today, say first: “It’s okay—we’ll sort it out together.”
71	Set aside one thing you want to “correct” in your spouse, and pray for them first
72	Today, take the initiative to share one of your spouse’s burdens
73	“Bear one another’s burdens” — Lord, right now let me lift people up rather than condemn them.
74	Gentle Wisdom from Above
75	A gentle home yields the fruit of righteousness sown in peace
76	Looking back over this week, was there a moment when you chose gentleness and the air at home changed? Quietly thank the Lord: “Lord, thank you for the work you’ve done in me this week. Keep making me gentle.”
77	321 Light: From a human point of view, only force gets results and only the loud get heard; from God’s point of view, the gentle inherit the earth and a gentle answer turns away wrath. Change your mindset and your life will change; a changed life changes how we live, and the relationships in our home change with it.
78	When the crowd wanted to stone the woman caught in adultery, Jesus neither went along with them nor excused her sin. He said to her, “Neither do I condemn you. Go your way. From now on, sin no more” (John 8:11). Jesus’ gentleness holds both grace and truth; with gentleness, he brings people back to the right path.
79	God who gives wisdom, thank you for teaching us about gentleness this week. Give us the wisdom from above, so that we are peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits. May we sow in peace in our home and reap the fruit of righteousness, so that family and friends sense the gentleness of Jesus in our home. Amen.
80	Take two identical plots of land: one person plants thorns, another plants fruit trees, and a few years later the scenery is completely different. James says, “Now the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace” (James 3:18). Every word we say at home, every tone of voice, is a seed. Gentleness is a kind of sowing, and its harvest is a peaceful home.
81	James asks, “Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show by his good conduct that his deeds are done in gentleness of wisdom” (James 3:13). Interestingly, he ties wisdom and gentleness together. Earthly cleverness is often about winning arguments and speaking impressively; but the wisdom from above is “peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits” (James 3:17). In marriage, winning the argument but losing the relationship is not wisdom.
82	This week we have seen that gentleness is not weakness but strength governed by God; that a gentle answer turns away wrath; that gentleness is learned from Jesus; and that when our spouse falls, gentleness restores rather than condemns. Gentleness takes time to grow, just as a fruit tree doesn’t grow up overnight. But as long as we keep sharing Jesus’ yoke, the climate in our home will gradually change.
