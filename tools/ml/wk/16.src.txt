0	Fruit of the Spirit (4): Patience
1	Planting a Pot of Waiting
2	Go together to buy a packet of seeds or a small seedling—pick a plant you both like.
3	Plant it in a pot together and stick a small card on the pot: “Leave it this year also—we’ll wait together.”
4	Each of you name one way you’ve seen the other slowly grow over the past few years, and thank them for their effort.
5	Agree to care for this plant together over the coming weeks, reminding each other: growth takes time.
6	Bearing with One Another in Love
7	Patience begins with humility and bears with one another in love
8	Think of the little habit of your spouse’s that makes you sigh most often. Don’t rush to criticize. Simply place it before the Lord alongside one of your own little faults, and say: “Lord, we both need you to bear with us.”
9	321 Light: Impatience often comes from making myself the standard and expecting my spouse to live at my pace—that is the pride of the self-ruled life. Humility means returning to our rightful place: I am not my spouse’s lord; the Lord is. When I let people be people, and let God be God, I can bear with my spouse instead of trying to remake them.
10	When the disciples couldn’t drive out a demon and the crowd was arguing, Jesus said, “How long shall I bear with you? Bring him to me” (Mark 9:19). Jesus really did sigh, but his sigh didn’t turn into giving up; it turned into action: “Bring him to me.” This is Jesus’ long-fused love.
11	Lord, thank you for always bearing with us. We confess that we often make ourselves the standard and lack patience with our spouse. Give us hearts of humility, gentleness, and patience, so that we bear with one another in love and eagerly keep the unity you have given, never letting small things tear down our home. Amen.
12	Paul writes, “with all lowliness and humility, with patience, bearing with one another in love” (Ephesians 4:2). These words are like a ring of keys, and the first key is humility. Why does patience begin with humility? Because what we find hardest to bear is often not another person’s wrongdoing, but that they “didn’t do it my way.” When I see that others need to be patient with me too, I find it easier to be patient with them.
13	The Greek word for patience is made of two parts, “long” and “temper”: a long fuse, slow to flare up. “Bearing with” means carrying, shouldering a load. Many things in marriage are not sins, just differences: how you squeeze the toothpaste, how fast you get out the door, the pace of your conversation. The best way to handle these is not to keep correcting, but to bear with them in love, like picking up a light piece of luggage.
14	Paul goes on: “being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace” (Ephesians 4:3). Unity is something the Spirit has already given; our job is to be eager to keep it. Patience is one way we keep that unity: we don’t let a small habit tear down a great marriage. A longer fuse makes for a roomier home.
15	The thing about my spouse that makes me most impatient—is it sin, or is it just “different from me”?
16	In what ways has my spouse been quietly bearing with me?
17	Is there a little habit of mine that you’ve been putting up with? Thank you. Would you be willing to tell me gently?
18	What things between us are “just different, not right or wrong,” which we could stop arguing about starting today?
19	Today, choose not to correct one of your spouse’s little habits—just bear with it
20	Name one way your spouse bears with you, and thank them
21	When you feel impatient, take three deep breaths before you speak
22	“with all lowliness and humility, with patience, bearing with one another in love” — Lord, right now please lengthen my fuse and widen my heart.
23	God’s Patience with Me
24	Only those who know God’s long patience can learn to be patient with others
25	Think back over your life since you came to faith—how many times has God waited for you to turn back, waited for you to grow? Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, thank you for not giving up on me.” Let this gratitude slowly fill your heart.
26	321 Light: The self-ruled life is lenient with itself and strict with others: my mistakes have reasons; theirs reveal their character. The self-emptied life turns this around and first sees how great a debt it owes God. When I shift from a human point of view to God’s point of view, I discover that being borne with by God is already grace.
27	Looking over Jerusalem, Jesus said, “How often I wanted to gather your children together, like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings” (Luke 13:34). “How often” speaks of his repeated waiting; even when the people were unwilling, his heart stayed open. This is the heart of God’s long patience.
28	Gracious and merciful God, thank you for being slow to anger and waiting for us to turn back time after time. We confess that we are lenient with ourselves and strict with our spouse. Help us always remember your patience with us, so that we see our spouse through the same eyes and give each other room to grow in love. Amen.
29	In Nehemiah’s day, the people looked back on their history, admitted that their ancestors had rebelled again and again, and then said: “But you are a God ready to pardon, gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and didn’t forsake them” (Nehemiah 9:17). “Slow to anger” in Hebrew is literally “long of nose.” The Hebrews pictured anger as steam coming out of the nostrils; a long nose means anger takes a long time to come out.
30	Peter says, “The Lord is not slow concerning his promise, as some count slowness; but he is patient with us” (2 Peter 3:9). We often feel God is moving too slowly, but in fact he is giving people time, because he wants “that all should come to repentance.” God’s patience is not permissiveness; it is love giving people room to turn around.
31	Patience in marriage is first of all not a technique but a memory: remembering how patient God has been with me. When I say to my spouse, “I’ve told you a hundred times,” perhaps I could ask: how many hundreds of times has God told me the same thing? Someone who often remembers God’s patience with them will slowly grow a heart that is patient with others.
32	On my faith journey, which of my weaknesses has God been patient with the longest?
33	When I compare what I’m impatient with in my spouse to God’s patience with me, how does it feel?
34	What do you think is the clearest time God has “waited” for you?
35	In what area would you like me to give you more time instead of pushing you?
36	Write down one thing God has been patient with you about for a long time, and thank him
37	Today, say “How many times have I told you?” one less time
38	Pray for one thing your spouse is working hard to change
39	“gracious and merciful, slow to anger” — Lord, right now I remember how patient you are with me—help me be patient with others.
40	Leave It This Year Also
41	Patience is hopeful waiting as our spouse grows
42	Picture a freshly sown field. Nothing seems to be happening on the surface, but under the soil the seeds are sprouting. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, where I can’t see, you are at work.”
43	321 Light: Wanting my spouse to change on my timetable is trying to do God’s work in them for him. Let God be God: he decides the pace of growth. My place is the gardener, not the Creator. Changing a mindset takes eight steps, with repeating and correcting along the way, and all of that takes time. We ourselves came the same way.
44	When Jesus healed the blind man at Bethsaida, after the first touch the man said, “I see men, but I see them like walking trees” (Mark 8:24). Jesus wasn’t disappointed; he laid his hands on him again, until he saw everything clearly. Jesus shows us that some change comes in stages, and he is willing to walk with us step by step.
45	Faithful Lord, thank you for never giving up on us and for giving us time to grow again and again. Give us the patience of the vine dresser—not pushing, not giving up, but tending, watering, and waiting together. Help us notice each other’s small steps forward, and place our timetable for growth in your hands. Amen.
46	Jesus told a parable: a fig tree had borne no fruit for three years, and the owner said to cut it down. But the vine dresser said, “Lord, leave it alone this year also, until I dig around it and fertilize it” (Luke 13:8). The parable was first a call to repentance, but it also shows us God’s patient heart: one more year, and hands-on care to loosen the soil and feed it. Growth in marriage has its seasons too. Change in our spouse takes time—and so does change in us.
47	Much disappointment in marriage comes from one expectation: “I’ve told you, so you should change right away.” Yet when we try to change a habit ourselves, it often takes years. The vine dresser’s patience isn’t passive sitting; it’s active companionship: keep praying, keep affirming, keep saying “I noticed” when the other makes small progress. That is how we “dig around it and fertilize it” for our spouse.
48	The Teacher says, “Better is the end of a thing than its beginning. The patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit” (Ecclesiastes 7:8). The proud look only at the present and want results now; the patient look to the end, trusting that God’s work in their spouse isn’t finished yet. Patience means handing our spouse’s growth timetable back to God.
49	Which change in my spouse am I most impatient about—most tempted to “dig up the seed to check on it”?
50	Has my spouse made some small progress lately that I haven’t yet noticed or mentioned?
51	In what area are you working to grow right now? How would you like me to walk with you rather than push you?
52	Looking back over these years, what do you think has been each of our biggest areas of growth?
53	Today, name one small recent step forward your spouse has made
54	Pray once for an area in which your spouse is growing
55	Tend the plant you planted on the weekend, and remind yourself: growth takes time
56	“The patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit” — Lord, right now I place my spouse’s growth in your hands.
57	Patience Is Not Putting Up with Harm
58	Wise patience also knows when to protect ourselves
59	Be still and ask yourself honestly: “Am I safe in my home?” Whatever the answer, you can say it out loud before the Lord. He cares for you; he does not want you to carry this alone.
60	321 Light: True self-emptying does not mean letting others trample you; it means letting Jesus reign. Letting Jesus reign also means not letting sin reign in the home. The one who abuses needs not more patience but repentance and help; the one who is hurt needs not more patience but safety and protection.
61	When the people of Nazareth tried to throw Jesus off a cliff, “he, passing through the middle of them, went his way” (Luke 4:30). Jesus did not stay to let people harm him, and more than once he slipped away from danger because his time had not yet come. His example tells us that getting away from danger does not conflict with obeying God.
62	God our protector, thank you for valuing every person’s life and dignity. Give us patience with good sense—willing to forgive weakness, and wise enough to get away from harm. Protect everyone who is hurt or afraid in their own home. Open a way for them, and give them a safe place and help they can trust. Amen.
63	Proverbs says, “A prudent man sees danger and hides himself; but the simple pass on, and suffer for it” (Proverbs 22:3). The beauty of patience is a willingness to bear with a spouse’s weaknesses, oversights, and immaturity without keeping score. But biblical patience never asks anyone to stay in danger. The wise see danger and take cover first; this is not a lack of love but God-given good sense.
64	So we must say this very clearly: physical, verbal, sexual, and financial abuse and control are never things to be patiently endured. Hitting, threats, ongoing humiliation, restricting someone’s movements or money, sexual coercion—these are not “a spouse’s weaknesses”; they are harm. In these situations, continuing to put up with it is not spiritual; it is dangerous. Forgiveness does not mean staying in danger. Safety always comes first.
65	If you or someone you know is in a situation like this, please contact a trusted pastor, a counselor, or local professional help and protection services as soon as possible. Seeking help is not betraying your marriage, nor is it a lack of faith. When David faced Saul’s spear, “David fled and escaped that night” (1 Samuel 19:10), and Michal even helped him down through a window. Protecting life is exactly what God wants.
66	Have I ever misunderstood “patience” as “swallowing everything”? Where did that idea come from?
67	Is there a friend or family member near me who may be in an unsafe relationship and needs my care?
68	For us, what things can we bear with in love? And what are the lines we both agree must never be crossed?
69	When one of us feels hurt or afraid, how can we say so safely? (If your spouse can’t do this with you right now, or if you feel unsafe, please take this question to a trusted pastor or counselor.)
70	Note down the contact details of your church’s pastor and a local helpline
71	Pray for a friend who may be struggling, and reach out with care if needed
72	Together with your spouse, name one line “our home will never cross”
73	“A prudent man sees danger and hides himself” — Lord, right now give me wisdom to protect myself and those I love.
74	Looking to Jesus, Running with Perseverance
75	In the long race of marriage, fix our eyes on Jesus
76	Imagine you and your spouse running a marathon together, your legs already aching. Look up and see the Lord standing at the finish line, waiting for you. Quietly tell him: “Lord Jesus, we’re looking at you, and we’ll keep running.”
77	321 Light: Keep the focus on Jesus—not on yourself, not on others, not on circumstances. Staring at your spouse’s faults, you grow more tired with every mile; staring at your own grievances, you slow down; looking to Jesus, you find strength to keep going. When we turn from a temporary view to an eternal view, patience finds hope.
78	On the cross, Jesus endured the hostility and mockery of sinners. He could have come down, yet he chose to stay there until he said, “It is finished!” (John 19:30). His endurance was not being trapped; it was choosing to stay because he loved us. When we grow weary in marriage, he is the Lord who has already finished the race and is waiting for us at the finish line.
79	Lord Jesus, you are the author and perfecter of our faith. Thank you for enduring the cross because you love us. Help us lay aside old scores, bitterness, and unrealistic expectations, and run the road of marriage with perseverance. When we are weary and discouraged, let us look up to you and run to the finish together. Amen.
80	Hebrews says, “let’s run with perseverance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith” (Hebrews 12:1–2). Marriage is not a sprint but a marathon. A sprint takes explosive power; a marathon takes endurance. Many marriages don’t collapse in one great storm; they slowly run out of strength through years of accumulated weariness.
81	The writer reminds us that before we run, we must “lay aside every weight” (Hebrews 12:1). Marathon runners don’t run carrying luggage. In marriage, old scores, bitterness, comparison, and unrealistic expectations are all heavy luggage. The secret of patience is sometimes not trying harder but putting down what we don’t need to carry, so we can run lighter.
82	Most important of all is “looking to Jesus.” Jesus, “for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame” (Hebrews 12:2). His endurance had a direction, because he saw the joy ahead. This week we have learned that patience begins with humility, that we remember God’s patience, wait like a farmer, wisely protect safety, and look to Jesus to finish the race. Next week we taste the fifth flavor: kindness.
