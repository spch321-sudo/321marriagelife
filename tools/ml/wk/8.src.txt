0	No Longer I: Letting Christ Live in Our Home
1	Say “I Do” Once More
2	Prepare a quiet corner. Light a lamp or a candle, and set out your wedding photo or your wedding rings. Begin by reading Galatians 2:20 together, then pray quietly for one minute.
3	Take turns saying: “Over these eight weeks, God has shown me that the biggest way I live self-ruled is… I am willing to hand it over to the Lord.” The other person simply responds: “I’m with you.”
4	Face each other, hold hands, and take turns reading these vows aloud (you may begin with your spouse’s name): “Before God, I choose you again. I confess that I have made myself the center, and I ask you to forgive me. From today on, I will no longer live for myself; may Christ live in me. I want Jesus to reign in our home, and I will love you, honor you, and serve you with his love. In good times and hard times, in plenty and in want, in strength and in weakness, I will follow the Lord together with you, all my life, until we see him face to face. This is my covenant before God.”
5	Pray together, placing this covenant in God’s hands, and bless each other. If your spouse can’t join you for now, you can read the vows alone before the Lord, offering your own “I do” to God first, and pray a blessing over your spouse.
6	Emptied, Not Erased
7	Self-emptying is not losing yourself; it is entering into God’s limitlessness
8	Be still for a moment and picture an empty cup set beneath a spring. Tell the Lord: “Lord, I am willing to empty myself, not to become hollow, but to be filled by you.”
9	321 Light: Self-emptied does not mean empty; it means entering the limitless and living out of the limitless. Those who live self-ruled have only their own little bit; the self-emptied have all of God. To have the mind of Christ Jesus is to let his heart replace mine, so that my limited self lives inside the limitless God.
10	Jesus said, “the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing” (John 5:19). The Son of God himself says he “can do nothing.” That is self-emptying at its most complete, and precisely because of it, he accomplished all the Father’s work. Jesus shows us that complete dependence on the Father is limitless power.
11	Lord Jesus, you existed in the form of God, yet you emptied yourself, became a servant, and died on the cross for us. We want to have your mind, to stop grasping, and to empty ourselves. Fill us with your limitless life, so that we no longer live within our limited selves but within your abundance. May you be honored as the highest in our home. Amen.
12	Being “self-emptied” is often misunderstood as wiping yourself out: no personality, no voice, no needs. Yet the most complete self-emptying in the Bible is Jesus, “who, existing in the form of God, didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant” (Philippians 2:6–7). He did not stop being God; he simply refused to clutch his rights. Emptying yourself does not mean becoming nothing. It means letting go of grasping.
13	This is the exact opposite of the Eden scene from Week 5. Adam and Eve were human, yet reached to grasp a place “like God”; Jesus was God, yet did not grasp. Instead he stooped to become human, “becoming obedient to the point of death, yes, the death of the cross” (Philippians 2:8). The first Adam reached out to grab and lost paradise; the last Adam let go and emptied himself, and opened heaven’s door for us.
14	The story does not end at the cross: “Therefore God also highly exalted him” (Philippians 2:9). This is the mystery of the self-emptied life: the one who let go was lifted to the highest place. Self-emptying is not a zero; it is placing our limited selves into the limitless God. Only an emptied cup can be filled again and again by the spring. The more a husband and wife empty themselves, the more they will experience together God’s abundance, far beyond all they ask or imagine.
15	What misunderstanding or fear do I carry about being “self-emptied”? Am I afraid of losing myself, or of being taken advantage of?
16	Where am I still treating some right as “a thing to be grasped,” holding on tight and refusing to let go?
17	In what way do you think I’m becoming more like Jesus? Please give one specific example, and let’s thank God for it together.
18	If we both emptied ourselves more, how do you imagine our home would be different?
19	Let go of one “right” you have been insisting on
20	Pray in the morning: “Lord, empty me and fill me.”
21	Memorize Philippians 2:5
22	“Have this in your mind, which was also in Christ Jesus” — Lord Jesus, right now let your heart become my heart.
23	Crucified Together, Raised to New Life
24	Through the gospel, the old self has died and Christ lives in me
25	Read slowly: “it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.” Pause, then read it again, saying “I” a little more softly and “Christ” a little more strongly.
26	321 Light: You cannot fix the self-ruled life by yourself; the harder you try, the more self-ruled you become. Only through the gospel can we become self-emptied. Jesus’ death deals with our sinful nature and deeds; his resurrection gives new life. Change your mindset and your life will change: I am not an old self trying hard to be better, but a new person in Christ.
27	At Lazarus’ tomb, Jesus said to Martha, “I am the resurrection and the life” (John 11:25). Then he called out Lazarus, who had been dead for four days. Jesus shows us that he did not come to improve dead people but to raise them to life. Even love in our home that seems to have died can come alive again in him.
28	Lord Jesus, you love us and gave yourself for us. Thank you that we have been crucified with you and raised with you. We will no longer rely on our own effort to become better; we believe that you live in us. Show newness of life in our home: in the kitchen, in the living room, in every moment we are tempted to lose our temper, let your life be lived out through us. Amen.
29	“I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20). This is Paul’s testimony, and it is our memory verse this week. Notice the words “have been.” Being crucified with Christ is not a goal we strive to achieve; it is a fact already accomplished in Christ. Our practice is to believe that fact and live it out every day.
30	Why is the self-ruled life so hard to get rid of? Because trying to remove “self” by our own effort is just another form of self-rule, like trying to lift yourself off the ground by your own hair. Scripture says, “our old man was crucified with him, that the body of sin might be done away with” (Romans 6:6). Jesus’ death dealt with both our sinful nature and our sinful deeds; and “just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father” (Romans 6:4), Jesus’ resurrection gives us new life. The gospel is the only road to a self-emptied life.
31	What does this mean for marriage? Picture two old selves getting married: two egos fighting every day over who gets the throne. But when two people renewed by Christ live together, the home holds two lives in which “Christ lives in me.” Paul says we are to “walk in newness of life” (Romans 6:4). Newness of life is not only for church gatherings; it belongs in the kitchen, in the living room, and in every moment when we are about to lose our temper.
32	Have I been trying to get rid of my self-rule by my own effort? How has that gone?
33	At what specific moment today will I most need to remind myself: “It is no longer I who live”?
34	Which part of our marriage do you think most needs “newness of life”? Let’s hand it over to the risen Lord together.
35	Share a moment when you came to faith, or experienced God’s love more deeply. How did God change you then?
36	Write Galatians 2:20 on a sticky note and put it beside your mirror
37	When you feel your temper rising, say silently: “Not I, but Christ.”
38	Tell your spouse: “Thank you. I see Christ in you.”
39	“it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” — Lord, live in me right now, and love my family through me.
40	The Vine and the Branches
41	A self-emptied life remains in Christ and bears fruit naturally
42	Imagine yourself as a branch, quietly attached to the vine. A branch doesn’t have to strain; it only has to stay connected. Tell the Lord: “Lord, today I won’t rely on my own strength. I just want to remain in you.”
43	321 Light: Live from the heart by the Spirit, not from the head by yourself. Those living self-ruled think love has to be squeezed out of themselves, so the more they love, the drier they become. The self-emptied know they are branches, and their strength comes from the vine. When our focus is on Jesus and not on ourselves, fruit isn’t forced; it comes naturally.
44	“Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there” (Mark 1:35). The night before, he had healed many sick people; early the next morning, he went first to his Father. If Jesus, the Son of God, constantly sought fellowship with the Father, how much more do we need it? He shows us that remaining in the Father is the source of all service.
45	Lord Jesus, the true vine, we confess that we often leave you and try to love in our own strength, and we end up weary and dry. Today we choose to remain in you, and let your life flow into our hearts and our home. Make us bear much fruit of the Spirit, let your love flow through us to each other, and let our heavenly Father be glorified through our home. Amen.
46	A branch never grits its teeth to produce fruit. It does only one thing: it stays attached to the vine. The sap flows in naturally, and the fruit grows naturally. Jesus said, “I am the vine. You are the branches. He who remains in me and I in him bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing” (John 15:5). This is not an exaggeration; it is simply true. Apart from the Lord, we can’t keep up even the most basic love for long.
47	Many couples work very hard at loving, yet the more they love, the more exhausted they become. That is because they are living from the head by themselves, not from the heart by the Spirit. Live from the head and you fret; live from the heart and you find joy. The self-emptied life is not about trying harder but about staying connected: returning to God’s Word every day, handing ourselves over to the Lord in prayer, and letting the Holy Spirit guide every reaction. A branch has only one job: to remain.
48	Jesus said, “In this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit” (John 15:8). The fruit in a marriage is the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience… and that is exactly what we will learn together in the next stage. This is what 920 (my spouse is my most important “2”; practice the “0” of self-emptying; bear the “9”-fold fruit) looks like: we practice the self-emptied life (0) with our spouse, our most important “2,” we remain in the Lord, and the nine fruits of the Spirit (9) grow naturally, and our heavenly Father is glorified through our home.
49	Lately, has my way of loving my spouse looked more like a branch gritting its teeth, or a branch connected to the vine?
50	Where is my daily time for “remaining in the Lord”? What is crowding it out?
51	How can we help each other so that we each have time alone with the Lord every day?
52	When have you felt that the two of us were most deeply “connected to the Lord” together? How could we have more moments like that?
53	Take five minutes to read Scripture and pray quietly, doing nothing else
54	Protect an uninterrupted devotional time for your spouse

57	The Dividing Wall Comes Down
58	The cross put the hostility to death; only two self-emptied people can truly become one
59	Consider whether there is an invisible wall between the two of you right now: something left unresolved, a subject neither of you wants to bring up. You don’t need to rush to tear it down. First bring it to the cross and tell the Lord: “Lord, you are our peace.”
60	321 Light: Only the self-emptied can become one. Two self-ruled people are like two circles with two centers, forever pulling against each other; two self-emptied people share Jesus as one center, and the closer they come to him, the closer they come to each other. When our focus is on Jesus, not on ourselves, not on others, not on our circumstances, the wall will one day fall.
61	Among the twelve disciples Jesus chose were Matthew, who had collected taxes for Rome, and Simon, who belonged to the Zealots. In most people’s eyes, they should have been sworn enemies; at Jesus’ side, they became companions. Jesus shows us that as long as he is at the center, even the two most unlikely people can walk together.
62	Lord Jesus, Prince of Peace, on the cross you put the hostility to death and broke down the dividing wall. We bring every brick between us to your cross. Take away our self-protective hearts, make you our one shared center, and make us one new person in you, living out a testimony of oneness. Amen.
63	When Paul wrote these words, he was talking about Jews and Gentiles: two groups hostile to each other, with deep, long-standing grievances. Yet Christ “is our peace, who made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation” (Ephesians 2:14). If even that kind of chasm can be bridged in Christ, then the wall between a husband and wife is not beyond tearing down in him.
64	The wall between spouses is usually built one brick at a time: a hurt left unspoken, a cold war, a “forget it, I don’t want to talk about it.” Behind every brick is a self trying to protect itself. This wall won’t fall because one side rams it with all their strength. It falls when both bring themselves to the cross, where Christ came to “reconcile them both in one body to God through the cross, having killed the hostility through it” (Ephesians 2:16).
65	Why do we say only two self-emptied people can become one? Because two self-ruled people can only negotiate terms, draw boundaries, and keep score; at best they coexist peacefully. Only when both let Christ reign do they share a common center. The passage says Christ came to “create in himself one new man of the two” (Ephesians 2:15). Oneness does not mean you become me or I become you. It means we both become more like Christ, and in him become a new “us.”
66	Is there a “brick” between us that I laid with my own hands? What is it?
67	In this matter, what do I need to lay down at the cross first, so that the wall can come down?
68	Is there a subject we keep avoiding? We don’t need to finish discussing it today; just say to each other: “I’m willing to find a time to face it together with you.”
69	When do you think we are most like “one new man,” united in heart and walking together?

74	Renewing Our Covenant Before the Lord
75	Renew your covenant as a living sacrifice, and let Christ reign in your home
76	Recall the sound of your voice saying “I do” on your wedding day. How much did you understand then? And how much more grace have you experienced since? Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, with the life I have today, I want to say ‘I do’ once more.”
77	321 Light: This brings the mindset stage to a close. Change your mindset and your life will change; change your life and your daily living will change. Renewing our covenant turns these eight weeks of new thinking into the direction of our lives: life first, then relationship. Let Jesus reign in our home, let Jesus receive all the glory, and together build a household that belongs to God.
78	At the Last Supper, Jesus took the cup and said, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you” (Luke 22:20). He did not just speak this covenant; he kept it with his life. Our marriage covenant can hold, not because our willpower is strong enough, but because the Lord who made the new covenant lives in us.
79	Lord Jesus, maker of the new covenant, thank you for making a covenant with us in your own blood and for keeping it faithfully. Today we once again present our bodies as a living sacrifice, and we offer our marriage to you anew. We will no longer live for ourselves; reign in our home and live in us. Strengthen our vows, so that together we may build a household that belongs to God and welcome your return. Amen.
80	Most of us spoke our wedding vows amid excitement and nerves. Back then we didn’t really know which days “in good times and hard times” would include. Reading our vows again is not because the old covenant has expired, but because today we understand better what we are promising. It is like an old song: when you’re young, you sing the melody; after the years have passed, you sing your life.
81	Paul urges us “to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God” (Romans 12:1). Someone once said the trouble with a living sacrifice is that it keeps crawling off the altar, so it has to be offered again every day. The marriage covenant is the same: not a one-time declaration at a wedding, but a daily renewal for a lifetime. The psalmist asks, “What will I give to the LORD for all his benefits toward me?” (Psalm 116:12) And he answers: “I will pay my vows to the LORD, yes, in the presence of all his people” (Psalm 116:14). A vow is a response to grace.
82	Over these eight weeks, we started from God’s design and looked at the self-ruled life in Eden, at pride and humility, at self-denial and self-emptying. This time our vows carry something new: not only “I love you,” but “It is no longer I, but Christ in me who loves you.” If for now you can only walk this road alone, you can still say “I do” before the Lord first. And a clear reminder: covenant commitment never requires anyone to stay in violence or danger. If you have any concerns about your safety, seek help from a pastor and from professionals first.
