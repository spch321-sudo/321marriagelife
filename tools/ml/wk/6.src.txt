0	Who Is on the Throne? Pride and Humility
1	Handing the Crown to Jesus
2	Read John 13:3–15 together, then pause in silence and picture Jesus picking up the towel and bending down.
3	Prepare a basin of warm water and a towel, and take turns washing each other’s hands (or feet, if you’re willing). As you wash, say: “Thank you. I want to serve you.”
4	Each name one area at home where you most want to “be king,” then say together: “Lord Jesus, please reign here.”
5	Read James 4:6 aloud together and bless each other: “May God’s grace flow into your heart, and into our home.”
6	Did I Build Great Babylon?
7	Pride means leaving our rightful place and taking God’s glory for ourselves
8	Take a deep breath, then slowly let it out. Think of everything you hold in your hands: your home, your work, your spouse, your children, your plans. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, all of this was given by you. I didn’t build it.”
9	321 Light: Pride puts me in God’s place; humility lays down power and returns to our rightful place. Let people be people, and let God be God. When I acknowledge that “this home was built by God,” I no longer need to trade my contributions for power, or demand that my spouse bow before my crown.
10	Jesus had all authority, yet he said: “For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me” (John 6:38). He never fought for glory for himself; he always gave the glory to the Father. Jesus is our example: the more capable we are, the more carefully we should keep to our rightful place.
11	King of heaven, we admit that we often treat what you have given as our own achievement, and that we have worn crowns at home that don’t belong to us. Forgive our pride. Help us lift our eyes to heaven and return to our rightful place. This home was built by you, and all the glory belongs to you. Rule and reign in our home. In the name of the Lord Jesus, amen.
12	King Nebuchadnezzar strolled through his palace, looked out over the city, and said: “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built for the royal dwelling place by the might of my power and for the glory of my majesty?” (Daniel 4:30). One short sentence, and it’s full of “I” and “my.” That is the very essence of pride: treating what God has given as something I earned, and taking God’s glory for myself. Pride isn’t always loud and showy; often it’s just a quiet thought in the heart: “This is all thanks to me.”
13	At home, pride says similar things: “If I weren’t holding this family together, it would have fallen apart long ago,” “I’m the one who earns the money,” “I’m the one who’s put up with everything all these years.” Some of this may be partly true, but when it becomes my crown, I start demanding that others listen to me, thank me, and obey me. Pride means leaving our rightful place, grabbing for power, and wanting to take God’s place. The result is just as Scripture says: “Pride goes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall” (Proverbs 16:18).
14	The king was driven out into the fields to live like an animal. But the story doesn’t end there. When the time was up, he says: “I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my understanding returned to me” (Daniel 4:34). At what moment did he regain his sanity? At the very moment he lifted his eyes to heaven. When a person looks up and acknowledges that there is a King in heaven, he returns to his rightful place as a human being and becomes truly human again. Humility isn’t putting ourselves down; it’s returning to our proper place.
15	In our home, is there a quiet “This is all thanks to me” in my heart? About what?
16	Have I ever used what I contribute as a bargaining chip to make my spouse do things my way?
17	Each say: “There’s one good thing in our home that is really God’s grace, not just my doing.” What is it?
18	Is there any area at home where, because of my “contributions,” you feel you have to tiptoe around my moods? Please tell me gently; I want to hear it.
19	Change one “I did this” into “Thank God for enabling me to do this”
20	Thank your spouse for one contribution you usually take for granted
21	When you pray, say: “Lord, this home was built by you.”
22	“Pride goes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall” — Lord, right now I lay down my crown. Please reign in my heart.
23	Biting and Devouring: Pride Provokes Pride
24	When pride fights pride, both husband and wife are wounded
25	Think back to your most recent argument. There’s no need to replay the details; just feel again the force in your heart that said, “I have to win.” Bring it before the Lord and say: “Lord, please calm the war in my heart.”
26	321 Light: When pride fights pride, two “selves” are fighting over the same throne. As soon as one steps down, the throne is free for Jesus to reign. Backing up first isn’t losing your dignity; it’s choosing the relationship. People matter more than tasks; the relationship matters more than winning.
27	When the mother of James and John asked for places of honor for her sons, “When the ten heard it, they were indignant with the two brothers” (Matthew 20:24). One person’s pride instantly stirred up indignation in ten others. Jesus didn’t join the argument. Instead he said: “whoever desires to become great among you shall be your servant” (Matthew 20:26). He ended the rivalry with the rules of his kingdom.
28	Lord who gives peace, we admit that we often speak in order to win and dig in to save face, and we end up hurting each other. Forgive us for biting and devouring each other, and set us free from the cycle of pride. Help us be the ones who back up first and serve each other in love, so that our home turns from a battlefield into a safe harbor. Amen.
29	Picture two cars meeting head-on in a narrow alley, and neither driver will back up. One honks, the other honks louder; one inches forward, the other inches forward too. In the end the two cars are bumper to bumper and nobody can get through. Proverbs captures the scene in a single line: “Pride only breeds quarrels” (Proverbs 13:10). In many couples’ fights, the problem isn’t that the issue is so hard to solve, but that neither person will back up.
30	Pride has one telling trait: it provokes pride in the other person. If I use a commanding tone, my spouse answers with a defiant tone; if I raise my voice, my spouse raises theirs; if I dredge up old grievances, my spouse dredges up even older ones. Paul says: “But if you bite and devour one another, be careful that you don’t consume one another” (Galatians 5:15). When husband and wife bite each other, there’s never a winner, because the wounds are inflicted on the same body.
31	The way out Paul gives is this: “through love be servants to one another” (Galatians 5:13). “One another” takes two people, but someone has to go first. The second half of the proverb says: “wisdom is with people who take advice” (Proverbs 13:10). The one who backs up first in that narrow alley isn’t the loser, but the one who lets both of them get home. Pride provokes pride and both lose; humility draws out humility and both win.
32	In our recent arguments, am I usually the one who “honks” first, or the one who “honks louder”?
33	On what kinds of topics am I least willing to back up first? What am I protecting?
34	What cycle do we fall into most often when we argue? (For example: one lectures, the other goes silent.) Let’s describe it together without working out who started it.
35	Next time we’re about to start fighting, what code word could we agree on to remind each other to “back up first”?
36	When you disagree, start by saying: “You have a point too.”
37	Agree with your spouse on a code word that means “let’s pause this argument”
38	When you notice yourself raising your voice, take a deep breath and slow down
39	“Pride only breeds quarrels, but wisdom is with people who take advice” — Lord, right now let me be the one who backs up first, not the one who fights.
40	God Gives Grace to the Humble
41	Humility draws out humility, and God’s grace flows into the home
42	Be still, and picture how water always flows to the lowest place. Tell the Lord: “Lord, I’m willing to lower myself and let your grace flow in.” Before him, you don’t need to hold it all together or prove anything.
43	321 Light: Humility is the key to revival. I don’t wait for my spouse to be humble before I am; change begins with me. When I humble myself before the Lord, I’m not losing to my spouse; I’m surrendering to God and letting Jesus reign, and grace begins to flow from me into the whole home.
44	When Jesus came to be baptized, John the Baptist tried to stop him: “I need to be baptized by you, and you come to me?” (Matthew 3:14). But Jesus said: “Allow it now” (Matthew 3:15). When humility meets humility, heaven opens and the Holy Spirit descends. Humility draws out humility, and heaven opens to relationships like that.
45	God who gives grace to the humble, we are willing to humble ourselves before you, submit to you, and draw near to you. Remove the pride in our hearts that sets us against you, and let your grace flow like water into our home. Help us lay ourselves down first, so that humility spreads between us, and our home experiences your revival. Amen.
46	Water never flows uphill; it always runs to the lowest place. God’s grace is the same: “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble” (James 4:6). The word “resists” in the original carries the sense of an army drawn up in battle against someone. The proud don’t simply miss out on grace; they put themselves in a position of opposing God. But a humble heart is like low ground, and God’s grace flows into it naturally.
47	James then lays out the path of humility: first, “Be subject therefore to God,” and then, “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you” (James 4:7–8). Humility is first toward God, then toward people. Someone who can kneel before God can also take the lower place before their spouse. At home, humility looks very concrete: being the first to apologize, the first to put down the phone and listen, the first to admit, “I don’t know. I need you.”
48	The most wonderful thing about humility is that it’s contagious. When one spouse lets go first, the other often softens too; when one says, “I was too impatient,” it’s easier for the other to say, “I was wrong too.” Humility draws out humility, and both win. And the promise is sure: “Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you” (James 4:10). A home that bows low before the Lord, the Lord himself will lift up. Humility is the key to revival.
49	Before God today, in what matter do I need to humble myself and stop trying to hold it all together?
50	In our home, which humble words are hardest for me to say: “I’m sorry,” “Thank you,” or “I need you”?
51	Recall a time when “humility drew out humility” between us: who let go first, and what happened next?
52	What specific way of showing humility would you most like to see from me? (For example: hearing you out before I speak, apologizing first, asking your opinion first.)
53	Take the initiative and say to your spouse: “I need you.”
54	On some small matter, ask your spouse’s opinion before deciding
55	Bow your head and pray quietly for one minute, handing your day to God
56	“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you” — Lord, right now I bow low before you. Let your grace flow into my heart.
57	Clothed with Humility: Let God Be God
58	Letting go of control and entrusting both our spouse and our worries to God
59	Clench your hands into fists and feel the force of your grip; then slowly open them, palms up. Tell the Lord: “Lord, I let go of my controlling hands, and I place my spouse and my worries into your hands.”
60	321 Light: Control is another face of self on the throne: on the surface it looks like caring, but underneath it says, “I’m in charge.” When I cast my worries on God, I no longer need to clutch at my spouse. When I let God be God, I’m free to be someone who loves, not someone who manages.
61	A rich young man came to ask the way to eternal life, and “Jesus looking at him loved him” (Mark 10:21), and told him the truth plainly. But the man went away sad. Jesus didn’t chase after him to force him, nor did he lower the standard. Jesus shows us that true love speaks the truth, respects the other person’s choice, and leaves the outcome to the Father.
62	Heavenly Father who cares for us, we admit that we often want to control each other because our hearts are full of worries. Today we cast all our worries on you, and place each other under your mighty hand. Teach us to clothe ourselves with humility, submitting to and honoring one another. And if there is anything unsafe between us, give us wisdom and courage to seek help. Amen.
63	Pride has a common but hard-to-spot form: control. We want to control our spouse’s habits, schedule, spiritual progress, even emotions. Behind control there is often worry: afraid he won’t change, afraid things will get out of hand, afraid the future will go badly. Peter speaks of humility and worry together: first, “Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God” (1 Peter 5:6), then, “casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you” (1 Peter 5:7).
64	When Peter says, “all of you clothe yourselves with humility” (1 Peter 5:5), the word he uses pictures tying on a servant’s apron. Humility isn’t just a feeling; it’s a garment we put on every day. Paul also reminds us “not to think of yourself more highly than you ought to think” (Romans 12:3). I am not my spouse’s Holy Spirit, nor his or her savior. My rightful place is to love and pray for my spouse; changing my spouse is God’s work.
65	To let people be people, and let God be God, is to place my spouse back in God’s hands: I do my part, and God does his work. Let it also be said clearly: the passage speaks of all of you who “subject yourselves to one another” (1 Peter 5:5): two people willingly honoring each other in the Lord, never one person controlling the other. If there is physical, verbal, sexual, or financial abuse and control in a marriage, that is not submission, and it is not something endurance can fix. Your safety comes first. Please seek help from a pastor and from professionals as soon as possible.
66	What part of my spouse’s life do I most want to control? Behind that control, what am I really worried about?
67	Am I trying to be my spouse’s “Holy Spirit” in some area?
68	When have you felt worn out by me “managing” you? I want to hear it, without defending myself.
69	Let’s each name one recent worry, and then pray together, handing it over to God.
70	Write down one thing you want to control, and next to it write “Given to God”
71	Today, resist the urge, at least once, to correct one of your spouse’s little habits
72	Name one worry and ask your spouse to pray for you
73	“casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you” — Lord, right now I place both my spouse and my worries into your hands.
74	Picking Up the Towel: What Humility Looks Like
75	Only those who know whose they are can bend down to serve
76	Imagine the Lord Jesus carrying a basin of water and kneeling in front of you. Will you let him wash your feet? Quietly receive his love, then tell him: “Lord, teach me to love my family like this too.”
77	321 Light: Humility isn’t low self-worth; it’s knowing our identity in God, so we don’t need to fight for position. Only those who are loved and accepted in Christ are free to bend down. When I let Jesus reign, I can serve in peace; when I let Jesus receive all the glory, I don’t need to grab the credit.
78	Jesus, “having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end” (John 13:1). The feet he washed that night included those of Peter, who would deny him, and of Judas, who would betray him. Jesus’ humility doesn’t pick and choose; his love doesn’t depend on whether others deserve it, but flows from who he is.
79	Lord Jesus, thank you for picking up the towel for us, bending down to wash our feet, and even laying down your life for us. Let us stand secure in your love and know who we are, so that we stop fighting over who is greatest. Help us wash each other’s feet at home and serve each other in small things, so that our home reflects your humility and you receive all the glory. Amen.
80	Before John records the foot-washing, he writes something very important: “Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he came from God and was going to God” (John 13:3). Then he “arose from supper, and laid aside his outer garments. He took a towel and wrapped a towel around his waist” (John 13:4). Jesus bent down not because he lacked status, but because he knew exactly who he was. Security is the soil in which humility grows.
81	In that culture, washing feet was the job of the lowest servant. That evening, the disciples were still arguing about who was the greatest, and no one was willing to pick up the towel. It looks a lot like everyday life in many homes: the dishes in the sink, the family member who wakes in the night needing care, the trash that needs taking out. Everyone is waiting for “the person who’s supposed to do it.” But Jesus answered the question of who is greatest with action: the one willing to bend down.
82	After washing their feet, Jesus said: “If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet” (John 13:14). Notice: “one another.” The most beautiful picture of humility in marriage isn’t one person forever bending down, but both people picking up the towel. When both are eager to serve, there’s no more need to fight over who’s greatest. This week we’ve seen how pride tears down and how humility builds up; now it’s our turn to pick up the towel.
