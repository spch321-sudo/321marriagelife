0	Love Does Not Seek Its Own Way
1	Tear Up the Old Ledger
2	Find a quiet little café. Each take a sheet of paper and privately write down one “old account” you’ve been holding on to in your heart and haven’t truly let go of. You don’t need to show it to each other.
3	Read Psalm 103:12 together. Each of you silently hand that paper over to the Lord in your heart, then tear the papers into pieces together and throw them away.
4	Take turns saying: “This week, for your good, I want to…”
5	On the way home, sincerely congratulate your spouse on a recent achievement or effort.
6	Love Doesn’t Envy: Rejoicing in Your Success
7	Love can truly rejoice in our spouse’s good instead of secretly comparing
8	Think of a recent moment when your spouse was praised or recognized. Honestly examine what your first reaction was in that moment. Tell the Lord: “Lord, search my heart for any comparison and envy.”
9	321 Light: Envy is the comparison of the self-ruled life: “Why them and not me?” The focus is on myself. The self-emptied know their place before God and don’t need to compete with their spouse for first place. When we let Jesus receive all the glory, we don’t have to fight each other for the applause.
10	When the seventy disciples returned from their mission and joyfully reported the results, “In that same hour, Jesus rejoiced in the Holy Spirit” (Luke 10:21). Jesus did not feel threatened by his disciples’ fruitfulness; instead he rejoiced for them and gave thanks to the Father. He is our example of truly rejoicing in other people’s success.
11	Lord, search out the hidden comparison and envy in our hearts. We confess that we sometimes treat our spouse as a rival instead of a partner on the same team. Heal our insecurity, and help us, like Jonathan, to truly rejoice in each other’s good, giving the glory to you together in each other’s successes. Amen.
12	When King Saul returned to the city after a victory, the women sang: “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands” (1 Samuel 18:7). It should have been a day of national celebration, yet “Saul was very angry, and this saying displeased him,” and “Saul watched David from that day and forward” (1 Samuel 18:8–9). One comparison planted a lifetime of envy in his heart.
13	In marriage, envy often shows up in disguise: my spouse gets promoted, and I say congratulations while feeling sour inside; friends all praise the wife’s cooking, and the husband feels outshone; the children cling more to one parent, and the other quietly feels let down. The root of envy is treating our spouse as a competitor rather than a teammate on the same side.
14	The same chapter shows us another person: Jonathan. As the crown prince, he had more reason than anyone to envy David, yet he “loved him as his own soul” (1 Samuel 18:3). Paul says, “Rejoice with those who rejoice” (Romans 12:15). Truly rejoicing in someone else’s good is sometimes harder than weeping with those who weep. Husband and wife are one, so my spouse’s success is our success.
15	What recent good thing in my spouse’s life did I congratulate them on while feeling a little sour inside?
16	In some areas, have I been treating my spouse as a competitor?
17	Is there something worth celebrating in your life lately that I haven’t properly rejoiced with you about?
18	How could we build a habit of “celebrating each other” at home? For example, naming one thing the other person did really well each week.
19	Today, specifically praise one thing your spouse did well
20	Pray wholeheartedly once for one of your spouse’s dreams or goals
21	When you notice yourself comparing, immediately turn it into thanks to God
22	“Rejoice with those who rejoice” — Lord, right now I choose to rejoice sincerely in my spouse’s good.
23	Love Doesn’t Brag and Isn’t Proud
24	Love doesn’t prove itself by lifting itself up; love builds others up
25	Be still for a moment and imagine yourself standing before God, with no need to report your achievements or prove anything. Softly say, “God, be merciful to me.” Rest in those words.
26	321 Light: Pride means leaving our rightful place and lifting ourselves up, wanting to sit in God’s seat and judge others. Humility means returning to our rightful place and admitting that we too need grace. Pride provokes pride, and couples end up competing over who is more right; humility inspires humility, and one “I was wrong” often brings back another: “I wasn’t right either.”
27	Jesus, the Son of God, said, “But I don’t seek my own glory. There is one who seeks and judges” (John 8:50). He had no need to promote himself, because he rested in the Father’s approval. Jesus shows us that true security frees us from the need to brag.
28	God, be merciful to us. We confess that we often lift ourselves up and try to prove to our spouse that we are right. Pop the balloon of pride in our hearts, bring us back to our rightful place, and make us willing to say “Thank you,” “You’re right,” and “I was wrong.” Let our words become love that builds each other up. Amen.
29	The bigger you blow up a balloon, the more impressive it looks, yet it holds nothing but air, and a single pin can pop it. Paul says love “is not proud” (1 Corinthians 13:4). The Greek word means “puffed up” or inflated, the same word Paul uses in 1 Corinthians 8:1, where he says, “Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up” (1 Corinthians 8:1). Pride inflates the self; love builds other people up.
30	Jesus told a parable about two men who went up to the temple to pray. The Pharisee said, “God, I thank you that I am not like the rest of men” (Luke 18:11), and went on to list his record of fasting and giving. But the tax collector stood far off and said, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner!” (Luke 18:13). Jesus said it was the tax collector who went home justified.
31	In marriage, bragging often hides in little sentences: “If it weren’t for me, this family would have…” “See? I told you so.” “I know better than you.” These may even be true, but like the Pharisee’s prayer, they put me up high and my spouse down low. Love that doesn’t brag is willing to say, “Thank you,” “You’re right,” and “I was wrong this time.”
32	What “bragging sentence” do I say most often to my spouse?
33	In what area do I feel I “know better” than my spouse, and so often look down on their opinion?
34	Is there something I often say that makes you feel looked down on? Please tell me gently. I want to hear it.
35	In our home, what words do the most to “build you up” and give you strength?
36	Say “You’re right” to your spouse at least once today
37	When you catch yourself about to say “I told you so,” swallow the words
38	Say one specific thing that builds your spouse up
39	“love builds up” — Lord, right now help me build others up in love rather than lift myself up.
40	Love Doesn’t Seek Its Own Way
41	Love faces outward and looks first to our spouse’s needs
42	Think about the pressures or needs your spouse may be facing today. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, open my eyes today to see not only my own concerns, but theirs too.”
43	321 Light: When the self-ruled make a decision, the first question is “What’s in it for me?” The self-emptied first ask, “Is this good for us, and for my spouse?” This is not losing yourself; it is emptying yourself as Christ did, and in doing so gaining a greater life and a deeper oneness.
44	At the Last Supper, Jesus “arose from supper, and laid aside his outer garments. He took a towel and wrapped a towel around his waist” (John 13:4), and washed his disciples’ feet himself. This was a servant’s job, and none of the disciples was willing to do it. Jesus was Lord, yet he did not seek his own way; he bent down to serve.
45	Lord Jesus, you are Lord, yet you bent down to wash your disciples’ feet. We confess that we often look only to our own concerns and keep the good things for ourselves. Give us your heart, make us willing to look first to each other’s needs, to be considerate in small things and gentle in our words, and fill our home with the love of serving one another. Amen.
46	Picture two people sharing one umbrella in the rain. If each pulls the umbrella toward themselves, both end up half soaked; if each pushes it toward the other, the umbrella ends up in the middle and both stay covered. Love “doesn’t seek its own way” (1 Corinthians 13:5); it is the act of pushing the umbrella toward the other person.
47	In the same letter, Paul says, “Let no one seek his own, but each one his neighbor’s good” (1 Corinthians 10:24). He also urges the believers in Philippi: “each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others” (Philippians 2:4). Notice the words “not just” and “also.” Paul does not tell us to ignore ourselves entirely, only not to look out for ourselves alone. The eyes of love turn from “me” to “us.”
48	The same verse also says love “doesn’t behave itself inappropriately” (1 Corinthians 13:5), which carries the sense of rude or unbecoming conduct. Home is where we most easily become careless with people: a sharp tone, no regard for how the other feels. Love that doesn’t seek its own way shows care in small things: handing over the remote, saving the last piece for them, and even when tired, first asking how their day went. This is what it means to “Have this in your mind, which was also in Christ Jesus” (Philippians 2:5).
49	In which small matter in our home do I always pull the “umbrella” toward myself?
50	Is the tone I use with my spouse more careless than the tone I use with outsiders?
51	Is there something lately where you wish I would consider your needs more? Please tell me one specific thing.
52	Could we each choose one small thing at home this week where we deliberately “push the umbrella toward the other”?
53	Today, give up to your spouse some small thing or choice you would have liked
54	Ask your spouse: “Is there anything I can help you with today?”
55	Watch your tone, and treat your family with the courtesy you’d show a guest
56	“each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others” — Lord, right now open my eyes to see my spouse’s needs.
57	Love Is Not Provoked
58	Before I’m provoked, let God ask me: Is it right for you to be angry?
59	Think about the last time you lost your temper with your spouse. Don’t condemn yourself; just quietly bring that picture to the Lord and hear him gently ask: “Is it right for you to be angry?”
60	321 Light: Being quick to anger usually means “I” have been offended: my pride, my plans, my rights. Jonah’s anger came because God didn’t do things his way. Let God be God; when we admit he is wiser than we are, our anger loses the ground it stands on.
61	When a Samaritan village would not welcome Jesus, James and John wanted to call down fire from heaven. But Jesus “turned and rebuked them,” saying, “For the Son of Man didn’t come to destroy men’s lives, but to save them” (Luke 9:55–56). When Jesus was rejected, he was not provoked; he simply went on to another village.
62	Gracious and merciful God, you are slow to anger, yet we often explode at the slightest touch. Give us discretion to see the weariness and pain behind each other’s behavior. When anger rises, gently ask us: Is it right for you to be angry? Make us willing to stop and let your peace rule first. Amen.
63	When a pressure cooker’s valve keeps hissing, it means the pressure inside is already high. Love “is not provoked” (1 Corinthians 13:5); the original word means not easily stirred to anger. Paul isn’t saying love never feels anger, but that love doesn’t explode at the slightest touch. Often when we blow up at our spouse, it isn’t really about that small thing; the pressure inside was already full.
64	When Jonah saw the people of Nineveh repent and God hold back disaster, “it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry” (Jonah 4:1). He even said to God, “I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger” (Jonah 4:2). Jonah knew God was slow to anger, yet he himself was quick to anger. God didn’t argue with him; he asked only one question: “Is it right for you to be angry?” (Jonah 4:4)
65	Proverbs says, “The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger. It is his glory to overlook an offense” (Proverbs 19:11). Discretion means seeing more deeply and further ahead. When we see the weariness, fear, or pain behind our spouse’s behavior, our anger cools. Next time we are about to get angry, we can let God ask us the same question, giving ourselves a chance to look again from God’s point of view.
66	The last time I got angry at my spouse, what part of me was really offended?
67	What pressures are building up in my inner “pressure cooker” right now that I haven’t yet handed to God?
68	When I’m angry, what kind of response helps me calm down the most? What kind of response makes things worse?
69	Could we agree on a way that, when one of us is about to blow up, we pause first, each pray, and talk again a little later?
70	When you feel like getting angry today, first ask yourself: “Is it right for me to be angry?”
71	Write down one pressure that has been building up in your heart, and hand it to God
72	Agree with your spouse on a way to “pause and talk later”
73	“The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger” — Lord, right now give me discretion to see the needs behind people’s hearts.
74	Love Takes No Account of Evil
75	Love doesn’t dig up old accounts, because God has wiped our account clean
76	Look back over this week: not envying, not bragging, not seeking our own way, not being provoked. Now imagine God picking up the ledger full of your wrongs and throwing it into the deep sea. Quietly thank him: “Lord, you no longer remember my sins.”
77	321 Light: The ledger in my heart keeps me permanently in the creditor’s seat, free to demand payment from my spouse at any time; that is the power of the self-ruled life. Handing the ledger to God means laying down the authority to judge, and letting God be God. Only the self-emptied are truly free, and they set their spouse free as well.
78	Nailed to the cross, in the greatest agony, Jesus said, “Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing” (Luke 23:34). He did not keep a list of everyone who mocked him; he interceded for them. Jesus is our highest example of taking no account of evil.
79	God who forgives us, thank you for not dealing with us according to our sins, and for casting our sins into the depths of the sea. Help us hand the ledgers in our hearts over to you, to stop dragging up the past, and to stop using it as a weapon. And please protect those who are hurt in their marriages; give them safety, courage, and help. Amen.
80	Many shops keep a ledger that records exactly how much each customer owes, entry by entry. Paul says love “takes no account of evil” (1 Corinthians 13:5). The original word is an accounting term meaning to keep a record, to enter something in the books. What hurts most in a couple’s argument is often not today’s issue but the line: “You always do this! That time three years ago…” Dragging up the past means opening the ledger in our hearts.
81	The psalmist says, “He has not dealt with us according to our sins... As far as the east is from the west, so far has he removed our transgressions from us” (Psalm 103:10–12). Micah also says of God’s people, “You will cast all their sins into the depths of the sea” (Micah 7:19). God hasn’t forgotten; he has chosen never to bring them out again to collect on them. Taking no account of evil is also a choice: no longer using old accounts as weapons.
82	But taking no account of evil does not mean pretending nothing happened, and it certainly does not mean staying in danger. If your spouse keeps hurting or controlling you physically, verbally, sexually, or financially, that is not something to silently “not take account of.” Forgiveness means giving up the right to revenge, yet you can still set boundaries, protect your safety, and seek help from a pastor and from professionals. Safety comes first, and that too is love.
83	What old account am I still holding in my heart that I often bring out when we argue?
84	Of my sins that God has thrown into the deep sea, which one am I most grateful for?
85	Could we agree that when we argue, we’ll talk only about the one issue in front of us, and stop saying “You always” and “You never”?
86	Of this week’s five “love doesn’t” lessons, in which area do you think our family most needs to grow? If your spouse can’t join you for now, write down what you’d like to say to them.
87	Today, when you argue or feel upset, talk only about the one issue in front of you
88	Write down one old account you can’t let go of, pray over it, then tear it up
89	Memorize 1 Corinthians 13:5
90	“You will cast all their sins into the depths of the sea” — Lord, right now I hand the old accounts in my heart over to you too.
