0	The First Couple’s Breakdown: The Root of Self on the Throne
1	The Night We Take Off the Leaves
2	Find a quiet, uninterrupted stretch of time, put your phones in another room, and read Genesis 3:7–13 together.
3	Each write down the “fig leaf” you use most often (for example: silence, busyness, saying “I’m fine”). Take turns reading it aloud; the other responds only with: “Thank you for letting me see.”
4	Take turns finishing this sentence: “In our marriage, the part I’m willing to own as mine is...” The other adds nothing and makes no comment.
5	Hold hands and read Philippians 2:3 aloud together, then pray for each other: “Lord, please cover him/her, and cover our home.”
6	The Serpent’s Whisper: Wanting to Be God
7	The root of self on the throne is wanting to leave our rightful place and be God ourselves
8	Be still and notice which voice is loudest in your heart right now. Tell the Lord: “Lord, among all these voices, I want to listen to yours first.” Gently lay all of today’s doubts and discontent before him.
9	321 Light: Pride means leaving our rightful place, grabbing for power, and wanting to take God’s place. Every tug-of-war between husband and wife over “I get the final say” is an echo of that bite of fruit in Eden. To return to our rightful place is to admit that the standard of good and evil belongs to God, not to me. Let people be people, and let God be God.
10	The tempter came to Jesus too, provoking him to prove himself: “If you are the Son of God...” (Matthew 4:3). But Jesus answered: “Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds out of God’s mouth” (Matthew 4:4). The first Adam reached out and grabbed; the last Adam trusted and obeyed. Jesus shows us that victory begins with believing God’s word.
11	Heavenly Father, you are a generous God; you have given us far more than we lack. We admit that we often want to decide for ourselves what’s right and wrong, and that in our home we have sat in your seat. Forgive us. Help us step down from the throne and return to our rightful place. Let your word be the standard for our home, and reign among us. In the name of the Lord Jesus, amen.
12	Imagine a couple living in a house that lacks nothing, with just one drawer they’ve been told not to open. One day someone whispers in their ear: “He won’t let you open it because he’s afraid you’ll know more than he does.” From that moment on, they can no longer see anything good in the whole house; all they can see is that drawer. This is how the tragedy of Genesis 3 began.
13	The serpent’s first words were: “Has God really said, ‘You shall not eat of any tree of the garden’?” (Genesis 3:1). God had actually said they could eat from every tree in the garden except one; the serpent twisted a generous God into a stingy one. Then he spoke the real temptation: “you will be like God, knowing good and evil” (Genesis 3:5). This wasn’t just about craving a snack. It was wanting to decide for themselves what is good and what is evil: leaving their rightful place as creatures and becoming God themselves.
14	This is the root of self on the throne: making myself the center, wanting to be master and king. The text also says: “Then she gave some to her husband with her, and he ate it, too” (Genesis 3:6). Adam was right there, yet he said nothing. On the surface, conflicts in marriage are about chores, money, or children; dig deeper and it’s often the same issue: who gets the final say? When I insist that “my standard is the standard of good and evil,” I am actually sitting in God’s seat. Healing in marriage begins when I step down and let God be God.
15	In which recent matter at home has my heart really been saying, “My way is the right way”?
16	Is some voice of doubt, about God or about my spouse, making me see only “that drawer” and miss the good things God has already given?
17	In our home, which area most easily turns into a tug-of-war over “who gets the final say”? Let’s just describe the situation without criticizing each other.
18	Name one good thing God has already given our home that you’re thankful for, so we can turn our eyes together from what we lack to what we have.
19	Write down three good things God has already given your home
20	When you disagree, first say: “Let’s ask together what God wants.”
21	Notice one moment when you think “My way is right,” and hand it to the Lord
22	“Has God really said” — Lord, right now I choose to believe your word and not listen to the voice of doubt.
23	Fig Leaves: The Hiding Marriage
24	Sin makes us hide from God and from each other
25	Sit quietly for a moment and listen for that gentle call: “Where are you?” (Genesis 3:9). You don’t need to rush to explain, and you don’t need to hide. Just let the Lord see you as you really are right now. He comes to seek, not to shame.
26	321 Light: Hiding is the instinct to protect the “self”: afraid of losing face, afraid of being hurt, afraid of losing control. The self-emptied don’t need so many leaves, because their worth doesn’t depend on how others see them; they have been accepted in Christ. When our focus is on Jesus, we find the courage to be seen.
27	Zacchaeus climbed a sycamore tree and hid among the branches to watch. But Jesus stopped beneath the tree and called him by name: “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house” (Luke 19:5). Jesus didn’t expose his sin first; he accepted him as a person first. Only someone who knows he is accepted dares to come down from the tree.
28	Lord who calls us, thank you for not waiting until we got better before coming to find us. We admit that we often cover ourselves with busyness, silence, and excuses, and that we have built walls between us. Give us courage to step out of the trees and stand unafraid before you; and make our home a place where we can safely be seen by each other. Amen.
29	After they ate the fruit, their eyes were indeed opened, but not to see God. Instead, “they both knew that they were naked. They sewed fig leaves together, and made coverings for themselves” (Genesis 3:7). Before this, nakedness had been an intimacy without fear; after this, it became a shame that needed covering. The very first thing the first couple did after sinning was to put a layer of covering between themselves.
30	Couples today have all kinds of fig leaves too: busyness to cover distance, jokes to cover hurt, “I’m fine” to cover disappointment, phones to cover conversations we don’t want to have. Leaves can cover things for a while, but they can’t cover the fear in our hearts. Adam said: “I was afraid, because I was naked; so I hid myself” (Genesis 3:10). Behind our hiding there is often the fear that once we’re seen, we won’t be accepted.
31	But notice that in this story it’s God who takes the initiative: “The LORD God called to the man, and said to him, ‘Where are you?’” (Genesis 3:9). The all-knowing God knew perfectly well where he was; he was inviting him to come out. Intimacy in marriage is restored the same way: not by waiting for my spouse to take off their leaves first, but by answering God’s call myself, stepping out of the trees, and showing my true self to God and to my spouse.
32	What “fig leaf” do I use most often in our marriage: busyness, silence, jokes, or a quick “I’m fine”?
33	What side of me am I most afraid for my spouse to see? Where does that fear come from?
34	When do you feel most free to be your real self with me? What was I doing at those times?
35	What could I do so that you feel even less need to hide from me? Please name one small, specific thing.
36	Lay down one “leaf”: tell your spouse one real feeling
37	During meals or conversations, put your phones in another room
38	Tell your spouse: “You can tell me the truth. I’ll listen well.”
39	“Where are you” — Lord, here I am. Right now I stop hiding. Please see me.
40	Shifting the Blame: From “Us” to “You and Me”
41	The self-ruled heart shifts responsibility, and blame turns oneness into opposition
42	Open your hands and rest them on your knees, as if laying down every finger pointed at someone else. Quietly tell the Lord: “Lord, today I won’t start by asking what others did wrong. I’ll listen first to what you say to me.”
43	321 Light: Shifting blame is the most common face of self on the throne: protecting my image matters more than repairing the relationship. Pride against pride leaves both sides wounded; when one person is the first to say “I was wrong,” humility draws out humility. People matter more than tasks; the relationship matters more than winning.
44	Adam pushed the blame onto his wife, but Jesus took on himself sin that wasn’t his. As he was nailed to the cross, he made no accusation; instead he prayed: “Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing” (Luke 23:34). The last Adam didn’t shift the blame; he carried it.
45	Lord, we admit that we are quick to see each other’s faults and slow to admit our own. Forgive our hearts that shift blame and accuse, and forgive us for even complaining about the spouse you gave us. In every conflict, help us find our own part first and bravely say “I was wrong,” so that our home turns from opponents back into partners. Amen.
46	God asked Adam a simple question: did you eat? But Adam’s answer took the long way around: “The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate it” (Genesis 3:12). In one sentence he blamed two parties: the woman, and the God who “gave” her to him. Eve went on: “The serpent deceived me, and I ate” (Genesis 3:13). Responsibility was like a hot potato, tossed to the next person in line.
47	Notice how the way Adam refers to his wife changes. Not long before, he had joyfully said she was “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23); now she has become “The woman whom you gave to be with me” (Genesis 3:12). Sin turned “us” into “you” and “me,” and oneness into opposition. Shifting blame is like pressing a split button in marriage: every time we say “This is all your fault,” we go from being on the same team to being opponents.
48	Many couples’ arguments are really contests over who is less wrong. But even when my spouse truly is in the wrong, my response is still my responsibility. Adam could perhaps say that Eve ate first, but that bite was his own. Change begins with me: not “You change first, then I will,” but “Lord, first let me see my part.” Owning my part is the first brick in rebuilding oneness.
49	In our most recent conflict, was what I most wanted to say “It’s all because you...”? What was my part in that situation?
50	Am I, like Adam, also pushing my discontent onto God: “You’re the one who gave me a spouse like this”?
51	Think of a small conflict between us (not the most painful one), and each say only: “In that situation, my part was...” The other just listens and adds nothing.
52	When you feel blamed by me, which of my ways of saying things hurts you most? I’m willing to learn to change.
53	Change one “It’s all your fault” into “I’m responsible too”
54	Over some small matter, be the first to say: “I’m sorry, that was my fault.”
55	Write down one thing you’ve blamed on circumstances or other people that was really yours to own
56	“What have you done” — Lord, right now I won’t push this onto others. I own my part.
57	Seeing Myself First: The Tax Collector’s Prayer
58	Seeing my own problems first, instead of fixing my eyes only on my spouse’s
59	Quietly bow your head, like the tax collector standing far off. There’s no need to list your spouse’s faults; simply tell the Lord: “God, search me, and let me see myself.”
60	321 Light: Moving from looking at others to looking at myself is the turning point from the self-ruled life to the self-emptied life. The self-ruled look at their spouse through a magnifying glass and at themselves through a flattering filter; the self-emptied let the Holy Spirit be their mirror. When I stop being busy trying to change my spouse, God has room to change both of us.
61	When Peter saw the miracle Jesus did, he fell down and said: “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord” (Luke 5:8). Jesus didn’t leave him. Instead he said, “Don’t be afraid” (Luke 5:10), and called him to go and catch people. Seeing our own unworthiness in Jesus’ presence isn’t the end; it’s the starting point of our calling.
62	God who searches the heart, we so often see each other’s problems but can’t see the state of our own hearts. Shine your light on us, so that like the tax collector we are honest and humble before you. And make our home a safe place, where admitting our weaknesses to each other is met with acceptance and support, not ridicule or the dredging up of old wrongs. Amen.
63	Imagine a mirror that can only reflect other people, never yourself. Many people carry a mirror like that in marriage: they see their spouse’s faults crystal clear, while their own problems always stay blurry. Jesus told this parable precisely “to certain people who were convinced of their own righteousness, and who despised all others” (Luke 18:9). As the Pharisee prayed, his eyes kept drifting sideways: “I am not like the rest of men... or even like this tax collector” (Luke 18:11).
64	The Pharisee did plenty of things: fasting and giving, both good things. His problem wasn’t his behavior but his comparing. When my heart says “At least I’m not like that,” I’ve turned my spouse into a backdrop to make myself look good. But the tax collector “wouldn’t even lift up his eyes to heaven” and said only: “God, be merciful to me, a sinner!” (Luke 18:13). Jesus said it was this man who went home justified. The renewal of a home also often begins with one person repenting like this.
65	The psalmist’s prayer is the best mirror: “Search me, God, and know my heart” (Psalm 139:23). Notice that he asks God to search “me,” not “him” or “her.” Seeing my own problems isn’t self-condemnation, because the One who searches me is also the One who will “lead me in the everlasting way” (Psalm 139:24). God shines his light on us in order to heal us.
66	When I think about the problems in our marriage, what comes to mind first: my spouse’s faults, or the places where I need to change?
67	If the Holy Spirit were to point out one thing in me today, what do I guess it would be?
68	Each finish this sentence: “One area where I’ve recently realized I need to grow in our marriage is...” Talk only about yourself, not your spouse.
69	When I admit my problems, how could you respond so that I feel safe? If your spouse can’t join you for now, write down one heartfelt thing you’d like to say to him or her.
70	When you pray today, pray only for your own change
71	Notice one moment when you’re comparing yourself with your spouse in your heart, and stop right away
72	Admit to your spouse one blind spot you’ve only recently noticed
73	“God, be merciful to me, a sinner” — Lord, right now I’m not looking at anyone else. I only ask you to have mercy on me.
74	From Fig Leaves to Garments of Skin
75	God himself covers us, so that we can count others better than ourselves
76	Looking back over this week, you’ve seen your own grasping, hiding, and blame-shifting. Now don’t stay there. Quietly lift your head, look to the God who made clothes for people with his own hands, and tell him: “Lord, thank you for covering me.”
77	321 Light: Trying to deal with self on the throne in our own strength only sews more leaves; only through the gospel can we move from the self-ruled life to the self-emptied life. Jesus’ death takes away my sin; Jesus’ resurrection gives me new life. Change your mindset and your life will change: I no longer need to win, because I am already loved.
78	Jesus told the parable of the prodigal son: “while he was still far off, his father saw him” (Luke 15:20) and ran to embrace him, then ordered: “Bring out the best robe and put it on him” (Luke 15:22). Jesus shows us the heart of the Father: he doesn’t wait for us to cover ourselves before accepting us; he himself puts the robe on the one who comes home.
79	Father of grace, thank you that when we failed, you didn’t cast us off but covered us with your own hands. Thank you, Lord Jesus, for laying down your life for us, so that we no longer need to prove ourselves. Help us to be humble in heart and count each other better than ourselves, and to cover each other with love just as you have covered us. Amen.
80	Genesis 3 doesn’t end with judgment; it ends with grace. Even while pronouncing the curse, God already promised: “between your offspring and her offspring. He will bruise your head” (Genesis 3:15). This is the Bible’s first announcement that a Savior would come. Then, “The LORD God made garments of animal skins for Adam and for his wife, and clothed them” (Genesis 3:21). The leaves people sew for themselves wither; only the clothes God makes with his own hands can truly cover.
81	Garments of skin meant that a life paid the price for them. This is a shadow of the gospel: Jesus’ death dealt with both our sinful nature and our sinful acts, and Jesus’ resurrection gives new life. We no longer need our own leaves to prove we’re right, because in Christ we have already been accepted. Only a person covered by God is free from constantly protecting themselves and trying to win every argument in marriage.
82	This is the power of this week’s memory verse: “in humility, each counting others better than himself” (Philippians 2:3). Counting others better than ourselves doesn’t mean pretending we’re terrible; it means that because my worth is already secure in God, I have room to value my spouse and help my spouse flourish. “each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others” (Philippians 2:4). Those whom God has covered learn to cover others too.
